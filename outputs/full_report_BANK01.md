### General Requirements

#### Understanding the Bank’s approach towards implementing IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 requirements

This report sets out Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s climate‑ and sustainability‑related financial disclosures. The disclosures are structured around the core areas reflected in IFRS S1 and IFRS S2: Governance, Strategy, Risk management, and Metrics and targets, with this General Requirements section providing the overarching basis of preparation.

The purpose of the report is to provide decision‑useful information on sustainability‑related and climate‑related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the Bank’s business model, cash flows, access to finance and cost of capital over the short, medium and long term. The report focuses in particular on:

- climate‑related transition and physical risks in the Bank’s own operations and financing activities;
- climate‑related opportunities arising from the transition to a low‑carbon economy; and
- the governance, risk management and performance frameworks through which these risks and opportunities are identified, assessed, managed and monitored.

The Bank uses IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 as the primary reference for structuring and describing these disclosures. The structure and content are designed to align with the concepts and disclosure areas in these standards, including the focus on sustainability‑related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the Bank’s prospects. At this stage, the Bank positions this report as an alignment exercise with IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 rather than a formal statement of full compliance.

For the purposes of this report, the Bank refers to short term, medium term and long term when describing the potential timing of climate‑related impacts. The Bank has not yet adopted formal, quantitative definitions of these time horizons specifically for sustainability‑related reporting or explicitly linked them to its internal strategic planning cycles. References to short, medium and long term therefore reflect indicative management views rather than codified planning horizons and may be refined in future reporting.

The Bank uses modelled estimates where primary data are not available, particularly for climate scenario analysis and financed emissions. Methodologies, data coverage and internal processes are expected to be refined over time as market practice, regulatory expectations and the European sustainability reporting environment evolve.

#### Fair presentation

The Bank aims to present climate‑ and sustainability‑related information in a transparent, balanced and decision‑useful manner. The disclosures are prepared using the Bank’s internal risk registers, value‑chain mapping, portfolio exposure information, climate scenario analysis and governance information.

In preparing this report, the Bank seeks to:

- describe material sustainability‑related and climate‑related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect its prospects;
- explain how these factors are considered within governance, strategy, risk management and performance measurement; and
- provide quantitative indicators and targets where data and methodologies are sufficiently developed.

There are important limitations to the information presented:

- financed emissions and climate scenario outputs are subject to methodological uncertainty, data gaps and evolving market practice;
- detailed calculation methodologies, data quality assessments and key assumptions for financed emissions and scenario analysis are not fully described in this report;
- operational Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas figures are not reproduced in this report; and
- the Bank currently obtains external assurance over selected Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas information; assurance does not extend to financed emissions or to this report as a whole.

The Bank does not assert that the information in this report is exhaustive or that all disclosures are fully comparable over time. Disclosures reflect the current state of systems, data and processes and are expected to be enhanced in future reporting cycles.

#### Connected information

The report is designed to show the connections between governance, strategy, risk management and metrics and targets, and how these elements collectively support the management of sustainability‑related and climate‑related risks and opportunities.

- Governance: The Governance section describes the roles of the Board and management in overseeing climate‑related matters, including the integration of climate considerations into decision‑making, the use of climate‑related key performance indicators, and the frequency and focus of Board and committee discussions.

- Strategy: The Strategy section explains how climate‑related risks and opportunities are considered in the Bank’s strategic planning, including the use of climate scenarios, the assessment of exposures to sectors such as mining, refined petroleum, air transport and selected manufacturing and service activities, and the identification of climate‑related opportunities in the lending and investment portfolio.

- Risk management: The Risk management section outlines how climate‑related risks are identified, assessed, monitored and managed within the Bank’s broader risk management framework. This includes the use of a climate risk register, the mapping of exposures across the value chain (own operations, upstream suppliers and downstream financing counterparties), and the consideration of both transition and physical risk channels.

- Metrics and targets: The Metrics and targets section presents key indicators and targets used to monitor climate‑related performance, including financed emissions, portfolio exposures to carbon‑intensive sectors, green finance metrics, climate‑related capital and operating expenditure, and climate‑related targets. These metrics are linked to the risk assessments and strategic priorities described in the Strategy and Risk management sections and are overseen through the governance structures described in the Governance section.

The Bank seeks, over time, to promote consistency between the data and assumptions used in this report and those used in its related financial reporting, particularly in relation to the presentation currency (euro). The systematic linkage between sustainability‑related disclosures and specific risks of material adjustment to asset and liability values in the next annual reporting period has not yet been fully developed or disclosed in this report. Users are encouraged to read this report alongside the Bank’s general purpose financial statements, including the consolidated financial statements of Eurolux Universal Bank AG, to obtain a more complete view of the Bank’s financial position and performance. The sustainability‑related and climate‑related financial disclosures in this report are intended to cover the same reporting period as those consolidated financial statements.

#### Reporting entity, business model and value chain

The reporting entity for this report is Eurolux Universal Bank AG, based in Germany. The presentation currency used for all monetary amounts in this report is euro (EUR), consistent with the Bank’s financial reporting.

The Bank reports:

- total assets of 850,000 million euro; and
- total loans of 31,150.64 million euro.

These figures provide context for the scale of the Bank’s balance sheet and the relative significance of the climate‑related exposures discussed in the Strategy, Risk management, and Metrics and targets sections.

The Bank provides commercial banking services. Further detail on the Bank’s business model is not described in this General Requirements section and may be addressed in other parts of the Bank’s corporate reporting.

For climate‑related purposes, the Bank’s value chain is mapped across:

- Own operations: including banking premises and related infrastructure, as well as the corporate fleet and business travel. These activities generate Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions and are exposed to potential physical disruption and transition‑related changes in energy use and mobility patterns.

- Upstream suppliers: including IT infrastructure and cloud service providers, office space landlords and professional services suppliers. These relationships give rise primarily to indirect environmental and social impacts and are relevant for selected Scope 3 categories. Not all upstream relationships are considered climate‑material; the Bank focuses on those suppliers and categories that are most relevant to its climate‑related objectives.

- Downstream activities and financing counterparties: including corporate borrowers in sectors such as manufacture of coke and refined petroleum, mining of coal and lignite, mining of metal ores, other mining and quarrying, air transport, manufacture of motor vehicles, manufacture of electrical equipment, manufacture of food products, civil engineering and education. These sectors are associated with varying degrees of transition and physical climate risk and represent key areas of focus for the Bank’s climate‑related risk assessment and financed emissions analysis. Within this mapping, the Bank distinguishes between nodes that are considered climate‑relevant and those that are not currently assessed as climate‑material.

The Bank’s value‑chain mapping highlights those nodes that are considered climate‑relevant, particularly where transition policy risk or chronic physical risk is significant. For example, lending to mining, refined petroleum and air transport sectors is characterised by elevated transition risk from carbon pricing and demand shifts, while exposures to certain manufacturing and service sectors are subject to moderate physical and transition risk.

Information on consolidation boundaries, the inclusion of subsidiaries and the alignment of the sustainability reporting boundary with the financial statement consolidation perimeter is not specified in this report. The Bank therefore does not state that the sustainability‑related financial disclosures are fully aligned with the consolidation scope of the financial statements and will consider providing further clarity on boundary alignment in future reporting cycles.

#### Statement of compliance

- identifies sustainability‑related and climate‑related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect its prospects;
- organises disclosures under the Governance, Strategy, Risk management, and Metrics and targets pillars; and
- seeks to provide material information that helps users understand the potential effects of these risks and opportunities on the Bank’s cash flows, access to finance and cost of capital over the short, medium and long term.

At this stage, the Bank does not assert full compliance with IFRS S1 and IFRS S2. #### Materiality assessment

The scope and focus of the climate‑ and sustainability‑related disclosures in this report are determined using a risk‑ and portfolio‑driven approach. The Bank applies a screening process that draws on:

- the climate risk register, which identifies key transition and physical risk drivers relevant to the Bank’s activities;
- portfolio exposure mapping by sector and value‑chain node, including high‑risk sectors such as mining, refined petroleum, air transport and selected manufacturing and service activities, as well as own operations and key upstream suppliers; and
- outputs from climate scenario analysis, which provide indicative views on how different transition and physical risk pathways could affect counterparties, sectors and the Bank’s overall risk profile.

This approach is used to identify sustainability‑related and climate‑related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the Bank’s prospects and to determine which topics and metrics are material for disclosure in this report. The Bank focuses on areas where:

- exposures are significant in absolute terms or relative to the Bank’s total assets and loan book;
- risk drivers are expected to be particularly sensitive to climate‑related policy, technology or market changes; or
- physical disruption or transition dynamics could have a meaningful impact on counterparties’ creditworthiness and, by extension, on the Bank’s risk profile.

No formal, standardised materiality matrix is presented in this report. The Bank does not describe or rely on stakeholder surveys or similar processes for the purposes of this General Requirements section. Instead, the materiality of topics is inferred from the Bank’s risk registers, value‑chain mapping, portfolio exposures and climate‑related metrics and targets.

The Bank recognises that its approach to materiality will continue to evolve. Over time, the Bank aims to further systematise its materiality assessment processes, enhance the integration of climate‑related considerations into enterprise‑wide risk and capital frameworks, and expand the range and depth of metrics used to monitor material sustainability‑related and climate‑related risks and opportunities.

### Governance

#### Overview

Eurolux Universal Bank AG integrates climate-related governance into its overall corporate governance framework. Climate-related governance is reflected in Board oversight, ESG & Sustainability Committee activity, executive management responsibility, semi-annual Board reporting, integration of climate considerations into the enterprise risk management framework, climate checks for major transactions and the use of a climate risk register.

The Board of Directors oversees climate-related risks and opportunities as part of its broader oversight of strategy, risk and performance. In 2024 the Board comprised 10 members, of whom 68.5% were independent. The Board met eight times during the year, compared with seven meetings in 2023 and four in 2022. Climate-related topics were included on the Board agenda in 72.6% of meetings in 2024 (73.1% in 2023 and 69.8% in 2022). Climate risk reporting is provided to the Board on a semi-annual basis and climate-related decisions were taken in 2024.

At committee level, an ESG & Sustainability Committee supports the Board’s oversight of climate-related matters. The committee met seven times in 2024, up from six meetings in 2023 and five in 2022, and considered climate-related topics during the year.

Management responsibility for climate-related risks and opportunities in 2024 is reflected in the role of the Climate Risk Management Committee. This committee supports the use of a climate risk register that, in 2024, recorded eight climate-related risks, and supports the integration of climate-related risks into the enterprise risk management framework and climate checks for major transactions. Six of the eight recorded risks are flagged as integrated into the Bank’s enterprise risk management framework.

Board skills and competencies for climate oversight are reflected in the proportion of directors with climate-related expertise, which increased from 33.5% in 2022 to 35.5% in 2023 and 37.5% in 2024, and in a skills development programme. Climate-related performance is also reflected in remuneration: the proportion of the Chief Executive Officer’s variable compensation linked to ESG factors increased from 6.3% in 2022 to 7.8% in 2023 and 9.3% in 2024, while the proportion of all executives with climate-linked remuneration elements was 8.7% in 2022, 15.3% in 2023 and 15.1% in 2024.

The Bank’s governance disclosures on climate-related risks and opportunities are intended to be read together with the Strategy, Risk Management, and Metrics and Targets sections of this report, which describe how the governance arrangements are implemented in practice.

#### The role of the Board of Directors

The Board of Directors of Eurolux Universal Bank AG has ultimate oversight of the Bank’s strategy, risk profile and long-term resilience, including climate-related risks and opportunities. In 2024 the Board comprised 10 members, with 68.5% classified as independent. This composition is intended to provide a balance of skills, experience and independent judgement relevant to the Bank’s business model and risk profile.

The Board met eight times during the financial year 2024, compared with seven meetings in 2023 and four in 2022. Climate-related topics were included on the agenda in 72.6% of Board meetings in 2024 (73.1% in 2023 and 69.8% in 2022). This agenda coverage reflects the Board’s ongoing consideration of climate-related risks and opportunities as part of its oversight of strategy, risk appetite and performance.

Climate risk reporting is provided to the Board on a semi-annual basis. This reporting provides an overview of material climate-related risks drawn from the climate risk register. Through this reporting and its regular agenda items, the Board is informed about climate-related developments and the status of key climate-related risks and opportunities.

The Board oversees the setting and monitoring of climate-related targets and related performance metrics through its consideration of management reports and climate-related decisions taken in 2024, which are summarised in the Governance decisions, controls and evidence boundaries subsection. These decisions also illustrate how the Board takes climate-related risks and opportunities into account when overseeing the Bank’s strategy, major decisions and risk management processes. The Board decision evidence does not describe quantified or specific trade-offs between climate-related considerations and other factors such as profitability, capital allocation, implementation cost, risk appetite or competing strategic priorities.

Formal climate-specific terms of reference or mandates for the Board are not presented separately in this report. The Bank does not currently disclose a standalone climate-specific Board charter; instead, climate-related oversight is integrated into the Board’s general responsibilities and activities as described above.

#### Board committees and climate-related oversight

The committee reports to the Board and provides a forum for more detailed consideration of ESG and climate-related topics, including risk, strategy, targets and disclosures, before matters are escalated to the full Board where appropriate.

The ESG & Sustainability Committee met seven times in 2024, compared with six meetings in 2023 and five in 2022. Climate-related topics were regularly included in the committee’s agenda during 2024, and the committee considered and prepared climate-related matters for Board consideration. The committee’s activities in 2024 included: reviewing climate-related risk information and management proposals; considering climate-related targets and performance indicators; and reviewing climate-related aspects of external reporting prior to Board approval. #### Management responsibility for climate-related risks and opportunities

Day-to-day management of climate-related risks and opportunities is supported by the Climate Risk Management Committee. This management-level committee prepares climate-related information for reporting to the Board and its committees and supports the use of the climate risk register and related risk monitoring.

The Climate Risk Management Committee provides climate risk reporting to the Board on a semi-annual basis. This reporting draws on the Bank’s climate risk register and provides an overview of material climate-related risks. The committee also supports the application of climate checks for major transactions, where climate-related considerations are assessed for significant transactions in line with internal procedures. The Bank records that climate checks are applied to major transactions; this does not constitute a formal mandatory policy disclosed in this report.

The Bank uses a climate risk register that, in 2024, recorded eight climate-related risks. The register records both physical and transition risk categories, including physical acute and physical chronic risks, as well as transition market, transition policy, transition reputational and transition technology risks. The register records risk ratings of high or medium and time horizons including short term (0–2 years), medium term (2–5 years) and long term (5 years and beyond).

Monitoring frequencies recorded for climate-related risks include quarterly and semi-annual monitoring. In 2024, six of the eight climate-related risks were flagged as integrated into the Bank’s enterprise risk management framework, indicating that these specific risks are considered within broader risk management processes. The Bank does not claim that all climate-related risks are fully integrated into enterprise-wide risk management beyond these recorded flags. Four of the eight climate-related risks were recorded as having changed compared with the prior period, reflecting updates to risk assessments and underlying assumptions.

Some of the climate-related risks in the register are linked to climate scenario analysis. In 2024, a subset of material risks was associated with specific scenario analysis work, providing traceability between those risks and the Bank’s climate scenario analysis, without implying that every risk in the register is linked to a scenario.

Mitigation actions are recorded in the climate risk register as labels associated with individual risks. In 2024, recorded mitigation action labels included climate scenario stress testing integrated into the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process, collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlays, engagement and transition-plan covenants with borrowers, green retrofit financing incentives, portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring, and sector exposure limits with enhanced due diligence. These labels illustrate the range of risk management, client engagement and portfolio measures considered in response to identified climate-related risks; they do not, by themselves, evidence that these measures are applied uniformly across all relevant activities or that every label applies to every risk.

The climate risk register includes material risk examples such as:

- Stranded asset risk related to fossil fuel sector exposure, classified as transition market risk with a high risk rating, long-term time horizon, quarterly monitoring, integration into the enterprise risk management framework and a recorded financial impact of EUR 136.5 million.
- Transition risk related to carbon pricing on industrial loans, classified as transition policy risk with a high risk rating, medium-term time horizon, quarterly monitoring, integration into the enterprise risk management framework and a recorded financial impact of EUR 67.5 million.
- Physical risk related to mortgage book flood exposure, classified as physical acute risk with a high risk rating, long-term time horizon, quarterly monitoring, integration into the enterprise risk management framework and a recorded financial impact of EUR 24.8 million.
- Reputational risk related to scrutiny of financed emissions, classified as transition reputational risk with a medium risk rating, medium-term time horizon, semi-annual monitoring, integration into the enterprise risk management framework and a recorded financial impact of EUR 65.5 million.

These examples illustrate how climate-related risks are recorded, categorised, rated, monitored and, where applicable, integrated into the enterprise risk management framework. The Bank does not map specific mitigation actions to these example risks in this report.

The Bank’s management governance records show that different management committees have been responsible for climate-related topics in prior years (Group Sustainability Committee in 2022 and ESG Executive Committee in 2023). ### Strategy

#### Overview

Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s climate-related strategy focuses on identifying and managing material physical and transition risks, capturing climate-related opportunities, and embedding these considerations into core banking activities and planning horizons.

The strategy spans the Bank’s value chain, covering upstream suppliers, own operations and downstream lending and capital markets activities with financing counterparties and customers. Climate factors are integrated into risk management through a climate risk register, sectoral exposure analysis and the use of climate scenarios within the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP). Transition planning and net-zero ambitions are guided by summary targets for own operations and financed emissions, with progress monitored using portfolio indicators such as high‑carbon and fossil‑fuel exposures, green lending volumes, financed emissions and portfolio carbon intensity.

Resource allocation decisions, including climate-related capital and operating expenditure, are informed by this strategic framework. Scenario analysis based on NGFS version 4 pathways is used to assess the resilience of the lending book under orderly, disorderly and hot house scenarios and to inform portfolio steering, client engagement, product development and capital planning.

#### Sustainability risks and opportunities across the value chain

The 2024 climate risk register identifies eight material climate-related risks, comprising three physical risks and five transition risks, assessed over short-term (0–2 years), medium-term (2–5 years) and long-term (more than 5 years) horizons.

Key physical risks include high flood exposure in the mortgage portfolio and wildfire risk in southern European lending. Both are classified as acute physical risks with high or medium ratings and potential financial impacts of EUR 24.8 million and EUR 22.7 million respectively over their assessed horizons.

Transition risks span technology, policy, market and reputational dimensions. High-rated risks include the shift to electric vehicles in auto lending (short term, potential impact of EUR 165.2 million), carbon pricing on industrial loans (medium term, EUR 67.5 million), and stranded assets in fossil fuel sector exposures (long term, EUR 136.5 million). Reputational risk from scrutiny of financed emissions is rated medium with a potential impact of EUR 65.5 million.

Across the value chain, the most material exposures arise in downstream financing counterparties. High‑carbon sector exposure represents 35.5% of the loan book, or EUR 11,059.49 million, and fossil‑fuel exposure accounts for 22.15%, or EUR 6,899.59 million. #### Climate resilience and scenario analysis

The Bank conducts climate scenario analysis using the NGFS version 4 framework across orderly, disorderly and hot house scenario types. Named scenarios include Net Zero 2050, Below 2°C, Divergent Net Zero, Delayed Transition, Nationally Determined Contributions and Current Policies. The analysis covers short‑term (2025), medium‑term (2030) and long‑term (2050) horizons and is applied to lending exposures in Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain.

The scenario methodology combines a top‑down sector‑pathway approach with sector revenue sensitivity analysis. Carbon price assumptions across scenarios range from approximately EUR 1.2 to EUR 287.1 per tonne of CO₂ equivalent. Technology readiness is varied across scenarios and sectors, and temperature outcomes range from about 1.5°C to 3.0°C above pre‑industrial levels.

In orderly scenarios, such as Net Zero 2050 and Below 2°C, the analysis covers the whole lending book across Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain. A top‑down sector‑pathway approach is applied, with carbon price trajectories mapped to counterparty sectors. Temperature outcomes in these scenarios are in the 1.5–1.8°C range and technology readiness is generally high, with rapid deployment of low‑carbon technologies. Under the Net Zero 2050 scenario, modelled estimates indicate stranded assets of up to EUR 33,575.2 million by 2050, derived from sector‑level fossil fuel capital expenditure exposure. In these orderly pathways, transition risk losses are assessed to remain within Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds, and green loan growth and client engagement on transition plans are expected to reduce high‑carbon sector concentration over the medium term. These resilience observations relate specifically to the orderly scenarios and their assumptions.

In disorderly scenarios, such as Divergent Net Zero and Delayed Transition, the analysis focuses on sub‑portfolios with heightened technology and policy risk, notably automotive and energy manufacturing lending exposures in Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain. Policy action is more abrupt and uneven, technology readiness varies from low to high across sectors and geographies, and temperature outcomes are typically in the 1.5–2.5°C range. Carbon prices differ by country, reflecting national emissions trading schemes and carbon tax trajectories. Modelled estimates indicate that maximum transition risk losses could reach 23.1% of capital in the short term under the Divergent Net Zero scenario. Under these conditions, near‑term resilience is assessed as acceptable, but medium‑term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is material. Sector exposure limits and portfolio decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring are key mechanisms to manage these risks, and additional capital buffers may be required after 2030 under the most adverse disorderly assumptions. These conclusions apply to the disorderly scenarios analysed and should not be generalised beyond them.

In hot house scenarios, including Current Policies and Nationally Determined Contributions, the analysis again covers the whole lending book across Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain. Policy action is delayed or insufficient, leading to higher temperature outcomes in the 2.5–3.0°C range. Carbon pricing ramps up later, particularly after 2030 in the Nationally Determined Contributions scenario, and renewable energy shares increase more slowly. Under the Current Policies scenario, modelled estimates indicate that maximum physical risk losses could reach 32.0% of capital by 2050, driven by increased flood, wildfire and heat stress impacts on collateral and borrowers. Under the Nationally Determined Contributions scenario, modelled revenue‑at‑risk estimates reach EUR 35,509.1 million in 2025, reflecting sector revenue sensitivity to higher carbon prices. In these hot house worlds, physical risk is the dominant long‑term challenge, with collateral revaluation risk in flood‑exposed mortgage books and agricultural borrower default risk as key transmission channels. The Bank’s flood‑zone overlays and energy performance certificate‑based collateral monitoring are mechanisms intended to manage these risks over the long term. These resilience observations are specific to the hot house scenarios and their assumptions.

Adaptation capacity is informed by financial resources, portfolio flexibility and investment in transition‑related activities. Climate‑related capital expenditure of EUR 476.95 million and operating expenditure of EUR 219.32 million in 2024 indicate that the Bank is allocating resources to climate risk management, operational efficiency and product development. Portfolio flexibility is supported by sector exposure limits, green loan growth, decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring, financed‑emissions and carbon‑intensity tracking, and client engagement on transition plans. These mechanisms are intended to support the Bank’s ability to adjust its portfolio under different climate pathways. The Bank does not currently disclose sufficient detail to assess operational adaptation capacity across all business lines.

### Risk management

#### Sustainability-related risk management overview

Eurolux Universal Bank AG identifies, assesses and monitors climate-related risks primarily through a dedicated climate risk register. For the financial year 2024, the register contains eight climate-related risks, comprising three physical risks and five transition risks. These risks are classified into the following categories: acute physical, chronic physical, transition market, transition policy, transition reputational and transition technology risks.

Each registered risk is characterised by a risk rating (high or medium), a time horizon (short term: 0–2 years; medium term: 2–5 years; long term: more than 5 years), an estimated financial impact, a monitoring frequency and, where applicable, a link to climate-related scenario analysis and specific mitigation actions. Four of the eight risks have been updated since the prior period, reflecting changes in the Bank’s assessment of their potential financial impact, time horizon or management approach.

The climate risk register is used as the central reference point for climate-related risk management and supports integration with the Bank’s broader risk management processes. Six of the eight registered climate risks are flagged as integrated into the enterprise risk management framework, representing 75% of the 2024 climate risk inventory. This integration is particularly relevant for high-impact risks such as transition risks in auto lending and industrial lending, stranded asset risk in the fossil fuel sector, reputational risk linked to financed emissions scrutiny and selected physical risks affecting the mortgage and agricultural portfolios.

Risk monitoring is structured through defined frequencies. High-impact risks, including transition risks related to the shift to electric vehicles in auto lending, stranded assets in the fossil fuel sector, carbon pricing on industrial loans and flood exposure in the mortgage book, are monitored on a quarterly basis. Other risks, such as wildfire exposure in the southern portfolio, building energy performance regulation and heat stress on agricultural borrowers, are monitored semi-annually. These monitoring frequencies are used to inform management oversight and to update the risk register where risk characteristics change.

Climate-related scenario analysis is used as a forward-looking input for selected risks. Five of the eight registered risks reference scenario analysis, drawing on a set of 18 climate-related scenarios based on the NGFS v4 framework. The Bank uses a range of orderly, disorderly and hot-house world scenarios, including “Below 2°C”, “Current Policies”, “Delayed Transition”, “Divergent Net Zero”, “Nationally Determined Contributions” and “Net Zero 2050”, with horizon years of 2025, 2030 and 2050. Four scenario identifiers (SCN0002, SCN0004, SCN0011 and SCN0013) are referenced by these risks, meaning that several risks may use the same scenario as an input to their assessment. Scenario analysis is used to inform the understanding of potential future credit and market impacts but is not treated as evidence of actual losses.

Mitigation actions are recorded at the level of individual risks in the register. For 2024, these actions include: “Climate scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP”, “Collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlay”, “Engagement and transition-plan covenants with borrowers”, “Green retrofit financing incentives”, “Portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring” and “Sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence”. These actions are used to manage specific risks and are not presented as uniform controls across all portfolios.

The Bank’s climate-related risk management processes, as reflected in the register, are linked to the broader sustainability strategy and to climate-related metrics and targets. Reported climate-related key performance indicators, including total financed emissions, carbon intensity, high-carbon sector exposure and fossil fuel exposure, provide contextual information for transition and reputational risks, particularly those related to financed emissions scrutiny and fossil fuel sector exposures.

#### Climate risk management

The climate risk register is the primary tool used by Eurolux Universal Bank AG to identify, assess, prioritise, monitor and manage climate-related risks. In 2024, the register covers eight climate-related risks, split into three physical risks and five transition risks, and provides a structured view across risk categories, time horizons, financial impacts, monitoring frequencies, scenario links and mitigation actions.

Climate-related risks are identified and categorised as:

- Physical acute risks, such as flood and wildfire exposure affecting mortgage and regional portfolios.
- Physical chronic risks, such as heat stress affecting agricultural borrowers.
- Transition market risks, such as stranded assets in the fossil fuel sector.
- Transition policy risks, such as carbon pricing on industrial loans and building energy performance regulation.
- Transition reputational risks, such as scrutiny of financed emissions.
- Transition technology risks, such as the shift to electric vehicles in auto lending.

Each identified risk is assigned a time horizon (short, medium or long term) and an estimated financial impact in EUR millions. This approach allows the Bank to distinguish near-term risks, such as the EV shift in auto lending and heat stress on agricultural borrowers, from longer-term risks, such as stranded assets in the fossil fuel sector and building energy performance regulation.

Risk assessment is based on a combination of qualitative and quantitative inputs, including:

- Risk rating: each risk is rated as high or medium.
- Time horizon: short (0–2 years), medium (2–5 years) or long (more than 5 years).

The Bank uses these parameters to identify top risks by financial impact. In 2024, the highest estimated financial impacts are associated with transition technology and transition market risks, followed by transition policy and reputational risks, and then physical risks affecting mortgage and agricultural portfolios. Four of the eight risks have changed since the prior period, indicating that the Bank updates its assessments as new information becomes available.

Processes to monitor climate-related risks

Monitoring frequencies are defined at the risk level. High-impact and strategically important risks are monitored quarterly, including:

#### Climate targets and progress

Eurolux Universal Bank AG has established climate-related targets that cover operational emissions, financed emissions intensity and a long-term net-zero objective. These targets are used to guide strategic decision-making, portfolio steering and internal performance monitoring. Oversight of climate-related targets by the Board and the ESG-focused Board committee is described in the Governance section of this report. Target-level internal owner roles and any linkages to remuneration are not separately detailed in this section.

The Bank’s principal climate-related targets are summarised below. All baseline values, target values and interim milestones are parameters recorded in the Bank’s internal target records and are not current-year operational emissions.

1. Operational Scope 1 and 2 emissions reduction target (TGT001)

- Target: Reduce absolute Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions.
- Scope: Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions, covering all Kyoto greenhouse gases, for the whole entity.
- Metric: Absolute emissions in tCO₂e.
- Baseline year and value: 2020 baseline of 137,849 tCO₂e (target-record parameter).
- Target year and reduction level: 42% reduction in absolute emissions by 2030 relative to the 2020 baseline, on a gross basis.
- Interim milestones: 
 – 2025 milestone of 120,480 tCO₂e. 
 – 2028 milestone of 97,321.4 tCO₂e. 
 These values are recorded as interim target parameters and do not represent reported or verified emissions for those years.
- Framework: The target record identifies the SBTi 1.5°C pathway as the target framework.
- Third-party validation: The target record indicates that third-party validation is recorded, with SBTi identified as the validation body.
- Progress: The target record reports 40.0% progress towards the 2030 reduction objective as at 2024, using absolute tCO₂e versus the baseline as the progress metric. This progress indicator has not been independently verified.

2. Financed emissions intensity reduction target (TGT002)

- Target: Reduce the carbon intensity of financed emissions.
- Scope: Scope 3 category 15 (financed emissions) for the whole entity, covering all Kyoto greenhouse gases.
- Metric: Emissions intensity in tCO₂e per EUR million of lending.
- Baseline year and value: 2022 baseline of 20.0 tCO₂e per EUR million of lending (target-record parameter).
- Target year and reduction level: 30% reduction in emissions intensity by 2030 relative to the 2022 baseline, on a gross basis.
- Interim milestones: 
 – 2025 milestone of 18.2 tCO₂e per EUR million of lending. 
 – 2028 milestone of 15.8 tCO₂e per EUR million of lending. 
 These values are recorded as interim target parameters and do not represent reported or verified portfolio intensities for those years.
- Framework: The target record identifies the Net-Zero Banking Alliance (NZBA) as the target framework.
- Sectoral approach: The target record indicates that a sectoral decarbonisation approach is applied.
- Third-party validation: The target record indicates that third-party validation is recorded, with UNEP FI identified as the validation body.
- Progress: The target record reports 25.0% progress towards the 2030 intensity reduction objective as at 2024, using tCO₂e per EUR million of lending versus the baseline as the progress metric. This progress indicator has not been independently verified.

3. Long-term net-zero greenhouse gas emissions target (TGT003)

- Target: Achieve net-zero greenhouse gas emissions.
- Scope: All scopes (Scope 1, Scope 2 and relevant Scope 3 categories) for the whole entity, covering all Kyoto greenhouse gases.
- Metric: Emissions in tCO₂e, expressed as a percentage reduction versus baseline.
- Baseline year and value: 2020 baseline of 120,000 tCO₂e (target-record parameter).
- Target year and reduction level: 100% net reduction in emissions by 2050 relative to the 2020 baseline.
- Relationship to gross target: The target record identifies an associated gross operational emissions reduction target (TGT001).
- Framework: The target record identifies the Net-Zero Banking Alliance (NZBA) as the target framework.
- Planned use of carbon credits: The target record indicates that the Bank plans to use carbon credits equivalent to 8.2% of the required reduction, with a focus on technology-based removal credits. These parameters relate to the design of the target and do not represent current or future purchases.
- Third-party validation: The target record indicates that third-party validation is recorded, with UNEP FI identified as the validation body.
- Progress: The target record reports 13.3% progress towards the 2050 net-zero objective as at 2024, using percentage reduction versus the baseline as the progress metric. This progress indicator has not been independently verified.

The target records also indicate that the operational emissions reduction target is reviewed on a triennial basis, the financed emissions intensity target is reviewed annually, and the net-zero target is reviewed every two years. These review frequencies are internal target-record attributes and do not imply any external validation of progress.

All references in this subsection to third-party validation relate solely to attributes recorded in the Bank’s target records. They do not verify current-year emissions, progress indicators, the consistency of the targets with any specific climate pathway, or the likelihood of future achievement.

#### Metrics and targets limitations and evidence boundaries

The Bank recognises several limitations and boundaries in its current climate-related metrics and targets disclosures:

- Operational greenhouse gas emissions: 
 Detailed 2024 operational Scope 1, Scope 2 and Scope 3 emissions records are unavailable. The Bank therefore does not disclose operational emissions for the reporting year and cannot provide a reconciliation between operational emissions and the operational targets described above.

- Financed emissions perimeter and methodology: 
 The Bank discloses a single financed emissions figure and a corresponding carbon intensity metric for its lending portfolio. The basis for the perimeter of the financed emissions calculation, including any specific asset classes or product types included or excluded, is not separately disclosed. Detailed calculation methodology, data quality assessments and key assumptions for financed emissions and related intensity metrics are not described in this report. Users of this report should therefore exercise caution when comparing these metrics with those of other institutions or across periods.

- Disaggregation of portfolio metrics: 
 Beyond the high-carbon and fossil-fuel exposure figures disclosed, the Bank does not currently provide a further breakdown of financed emissions or exposure by sector, geography, asset class or client segment in this section.

- Target metrics and progress indicators: 
 All baseline values, target values, interim milestones, progress percentages, framework references and third-party validation attributes are parameters recorded in the Bank’s internal target records. These parameters are not current-year operational emissions and have not been independently verified. The internal progress indicators do not constitute a forecast or guarantee that the targets will be met and should not be interpreted as external confirmation of performance.

- Third-party validation of targets: 
 References to third-party validation in the target records indicate that a validation body is recorded for the relevant target. These references do not extend to current-year emissions, financed emissions, portfolio intensity metrics, or any assessment of Paris alignment or future achievement of the targets.

- Assurance over metrics: 
 The climate-related metrics and targets disclosed in this section, including financed emissions, carbon intensity, exposure metrics, green lending volumes, climate-related capital and operating expenditure, and target progress indicators, have not been independently verified for the 2024 reporting year.

The Bank intends to enhance the scope, granularity and methodological transparency of its climate-related metrics and targets over time, including improving operational emissions data coverage, refining financed emissions methodologies and expanding disclosures on sectoral exposures and target progress, subject to data availability and evolving regulatory requirements.
