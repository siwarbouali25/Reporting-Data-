### Strategy

#### Climate-related risks and opportunities

Eurolux Universal Bank AG identifies both physical and transition climate-related risks across short-term (0–2 years), medium-term (2–5 years), and long-term (5+ years) horizons, based on its 2024 climate risk register and linked scenario analysis.

Physical risks primarily arise from acute and chronic climate hazards affecting collateral and counterparties. A high-rated long-term physical acute risk is associated with mortgage book flood exposure (risk ID RISK-BANK01-2024-0001), with an estimated financial impact of EUR 24.8 million. Mitigation actions include engagement and transition-plan covenants with borrowers, as well as the application of flood mapping overlays to collateral valuation, as informed by scenario SCN0004. A medium-rated short-term physical acute risk relates to wildfire exposure in the southern portfolio (RISK-BANK01-2024-0004), with an estimated financial impact of EUR 22.7 million. Mitigation for wildfire risk includes collateral revaluation and the use of flood mapping overlays where relevant for multi-hazard assessment, though the effectiveness of flood overlays for wildfire risk is limited and primarily supports broader collateral risk management. Chronic physical risk is also recognised in the value chain through exposures such as corporate borrowers in motor vehicle manufacturing and education, where operational disruption from changing climate patterns and heat stress can affect credit quality.

Transition risks are more numerous and financially material in the current assessment. A high-rated short-term technology transition risk (RISK-BANK01-2024-0006) arises from the rapid shift to electric vehicles in auto lending, with an estimated financial impact of EUR 165.2 million. Mitigation includes climate scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP, linked to scenario SCN0011. A high-rated long-term transition market risk (RISK-BANK01-2024-0005) is associated with stranded assets in the fossil fuel sector, with an estimated impact of EUR 136.5 million. Mitigation actions include collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlays; however, these actions are risk management tools and do not fully eliminate stranded asset risk, which remains material under adverse scenarios. A high-rated medium-term transition policy risk (RISK-BANK01-2024-0008) stems from carbon pricing on industrial loans, with an estimated financial impact of EUR 67.5 million; mitigation focuses on sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence.

Reputational transition risk is also considered material. A medium-rated medium-term risk (RISK-BANK01-2024-0007) relates to increased scrutiny of financed emissions. The bank’s financed emissions are currently estimated at 35,973,167.7 tCO2e, corresponding to a portfolio carbon intensity of 1,154.8 tCO2e per MEUR of lending. Mitigation focuses on portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring, linked to scenario SCN0013. These metrics, combined with the bank’s high-carbon sector exposure of 35.5% of the lending book (EUR 11,059.49 million) and fossil fuel exposure of 22.15% (EUR 6,899.59 million), are expected to continue to attract external scrutiny from investors, regulators, and civil society.

In parallel, the bank recognises climate-related opportunities across products, services, and resource efficiency, with a total estimated revenue impact of EUR 465.0 million over short-, medium-, and long-term horizons. These are modelled estimates, not guaranteed revenue outcomes. Key examples include:

- Expansion of green and sustainability-linked loan products aligned with the EU Taxonomy (OPP-BANK01-01), with an estimated medium-term revenue impact of EUR 180.0 million and medium confidence, linked to transition policy dynamics.
- Growth in sustainable bond underwriting and distribution (green and social bonds) (OPP-BANK01-02), with an estimated short-term revenue impact of EUR 95.0 million and medium confidence.
- Transition and climate risk advisory services for large corporates (OPP-BANK01-03), estimated to generate EUR 42.0 million in medium-term fee income with medium confidence.
- Renewable energy project finance for solar, wind, and battery storage aligned with REPowerEU and national renewable targets (OPP-BANK01-04), with an estimated long-term revenue impact of EUR 130.0 million and medium confidence.
- Operational energy-efficiency measures in own facilities (OPP-BANK01-05), expected to reduce energy costs and deliver an estimated short-term financial benefit of EUR 18.0 million with medium confidence.

These opportunities are strategically linked to the bank’s emissions-reduction targets and portfolio decarbonisation objectives described below.

The bank has adopted three key climate-related targets, each with specific parameters and validation:

- **Target TGT001**: Absolute reduction target for Scope 1 and 2 emissions, covering all Kyoto greenhouse gases, with a 2020 baseline of 137,849 tCO2e and a 2030 target of a 42% gross reduction under a 1.5°C-aligned SBTi framework. This target applies to the whole entity, is third-party validated by the SBTi, and is reviewed triennially. Interim milestones are set at 120,480 tCO2e by 2025 and 97,321.4 tCO2e by 2028. Progress as of 2024 is reported at 40% towards the 2030 goal. The latest assessment indicates that this target is Paris-aligned at the target level; this does not imply whole-bank or portfolio Paris alignment.
- **Target TGT002**: Intensity-reduction target for Scope 3 Category 15 (financed emissions), expressed in tCO2e per MEUR of lending, applying to the whole entity under the NZBA framework. The baseline intensity is 20.0 tCO2e per MEUR (2022), with a 2030 target of a 30% gross reduction. Interim milestones include 18.2 tCO2e per MEUR by 2025 and 15.8 tCO2e per MEUR by 2028. This target is sectoral-decarbonisation based, third-party validated by UNEP FI, reviewed annually, and progress in 2024 is reported at 25%. It is currently assessed as Paris-aligned at the target level; this does not imply whole-bank or portfolio Paris alignment. Note: The reported portfolio carbon intensity KPI (1,154.8 tCO2e per MEUR) differs from the TGT002 baseline (20.0 tCO2e per MEUR). This discrepancy may reflect differences in calculation boundary, portfolio coverage, or unit basis. Available documentation does not resolve this inconsistency, which is flagged as a current limitation to be addressed in future reporting.
- **Target TGT003**: Net-zero target for all scopes, covering all Kyoto gases on a net basis across Scope 1, 2, and 3, with a 2020 baseline of 120,000 tCO2e and a 2050 target of 100% reduction, aligned to the NZBA framework. It is associated with the gross Scope 1 and 2 target TGT001, applies to the whole entity, and is third-party validated by UNEP FI. The target anticipates the use of carbon credits for up to 8.2% of residual emissions in 2050, specifically technology-based carbon removal credits. Progress in 2024 is reported at 13.3% reduction versus baseline. This net-zero target is also currently assessed as Paris-aligned at the target level; this does not imply whole-bank or portfolio Paris alignment.

These targets, together with the identified risks and opportunities, form the central strategic framework guiding the bank’s climate-related actions.

#### Effects on business model and value chain

Detailed stand-alone business-model impact analysis is not available in the current source data; the following description therefore uses lending, portfolio, value chain, and financial evidence to describe observable transmission channels.

Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s core business model is centred on lending, capital markets intermediation, and related services, with total assets of EUR 850,000 million and a loan book of EUR 31,150.64 million. Climate-related risks and opportunities primarily transmit through the financing value chain and, to a lesser extent, the bank’s own operations and suppliers.

On the financing counterparty side, the value-chain map identifies several large and climate-material nodes:

- Corporate borrowers in the manufacture of coke and refined petroleum, with financial exposure of EUR 3,903.65 million, face elevated transition policy risk due to carbon pricing and demand shifts away from fossil fuels.
- Corporate borrowers in the manufacture of motor vehicles, with exposure of EUR 3,871.6 million, are exposed to both physical chronic risks (e.g., supply-chain and production disruption) and transition risks from the EV shift.
- Corporate borrowers in coal and lignite mining (EUR 3,547.58 million), metal ore mining (EUR 2,749.58 million), and other mining and quarrying (EUR 2,102.44 million) are exposed to high transition policy risk linked to carbon pricing and possible demand decline.

These exposures contribute to the bank’s overall high-carbon sector exposure of 35.5% of the lending book (EUR 11,059.49 million) and fossil fuel exposure of 22.15% (EUR 6,899.59 million). They represent key channels through which transition risks (market, policy, technology, and reputational) can affect the bank’s business model via credit losses, higher risk weights, and potential balance sheet reallocation away from high-emitting sectors toward low-carbon activities.

Downstream customers in sectors such as education (EUR 2,962.25 million) are subject to moderate physical and transition risk. While these sectors are not high carbon-intensive, chronic physical risk (e.g., heat stress, infrastructure disruption) can affect counterparties’ operational resilience and the bank’s credit risk profile, influencing lending conditions and pricing.

Within its own operations, the bank recognises physical and transition exposures. Operational sites and buildings are exposed to chronic physical risks and contribute to Scope 1 and 2 emissions, while the corporate fleet and business travel (Scope 3 Category 6) are exposed to transition market risk as low-carbon mobility becomes the norm. These operational exposures are directly addressed through the Scope 1 and 2 absolute reduction target (TGT001) and related energy-efficiency initiatives.

Upstream suppliers, particularly those providing energy, IT, and facilities management services, are indirectly affected by climate policy and technology shifts. While specific financial exposure data for upstream nodes are not provided, changes in supplier costs and reliability can affect the bank’s operating expenditure and service continuity.

Two value-chain nodes are identified as material but only qualitatively described, without quantified financial exposure. For these, climate risk transmission is recognised qualitatively, but the lack of financial quantification limits the precision of impact assessment.

Overall, the structure of the value chain—especially concentration in carbon-intensive corporate borrowers—means that climate-related transition risks have the potential to materially influence the composition of the lending portfolio, the relative contribution of high-carbon versus green revenues, and the bank’s engagement model with clients across key sectors. At the same time, the identified opportunities in green loans, sustainable bonds, transition advisory, and renewable project finance indicate a progressive shift in the business model towards climate-aligned products and services.
