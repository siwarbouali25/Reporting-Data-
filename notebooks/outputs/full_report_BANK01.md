### General Requirements

#### Understanding the Bank’s approach towards implementing IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 requirements

This report has been structured around IFRS S1- and IFRS S2-style sustainability-related and climate-related financial disclosure areas for Eurolux Universal Bank AG. It is intended to provide decision‑useful information on sustainability-related and climate-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the Bank’s business model, cash flows, access to finance and cost of capital over the short, medium and long term.

The disclosure follows the core architecture of IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards, organised into the pillars of Governance, Strategy, Risk management, and Metrics and targets. Within this structure, the report focuses on:

- sustainability-related and climate-related risks and opportunities relevant to a commercial bank, with particular emphasis on climate transition and physical risks in the financing portfolio and own operations; 
- the way these risks and opportunities are overseen by governance bodies and integrated into strategy, risk management and performance measurement; and 
- the links between climate-related exposures, scenario analysis, financed emissions and other climate metrics, and the Bank’s broader sustainability context.

The country code recorded in the available evidence is DE (Germany), and the reporting currency is euro (EUR). The specific reporting period is not specified in the available evidence. The report is prepared in the context of evolving sustainability reporting requirements that may apply to banks operating in Europe and is intended to align with the principles and structure of IFRS S1 and IFRS S2, without asserting full legal compliance with those Standards or with any specific jurisdictional adoption framework.

Parts of the report rely on modelled estimates, internally generated analytical outputs and available source data, particularly for climate scenario analysis and financed emissions.

#### Fair presentation

The sustainability-related and climate-related financial information in this report has been prepared using the evidence currently available for Eurolux Universal Bank AG, including:

- governance information, board and management oversight indicators and climate-related decision records; 
- a climate risk register and climate scenario analysis outputs; 
- value chain mapping and portfolio exposure data by sector and climate theme; and 
- climate-related metrics and targets, including financed emissions and selected trend indicators.

The Bank aims to present the information in a transparent, balanced and decision‑oriented manner. Where quantitative information is based on estimates, scenario analysis or partial data coverage, this is reflected through narrative explanation in the relevant sections. Data and assumptions used for sustainability-related disclosures are intended to be consistent, to the extent possible based on the available evidence, with those used for financial planning and risk management, recognising that detailed alignment with the related financial statements is not documented in the evidence set.

The report does not claim that all sustainability-related and climate-related risks and opportunities have been exhaustively identified or quantified. In particular:

- detailed calculation methodologies, data quality assessments and assumptions for certain metrics, including financed emissions and climate-related capital allocation, are not fully described in the available evidence; 
- external assurance is not in place for the report as a whole; and 
- assurance evidence, where available, relates only to selected Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions and does not extend to financed emissions.

Governance disclosures are based on the governance evidence currently available and are subject to the evidence boundaries described in the Governance section. As sustainability-related reporting practices, data systems and methodologies evolve, the Bank expects that future disclosures will be refined and expanded.

### Strategy

#### Overview

Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s climate-related strategy is structured around identifying and managing material physical and transition risks across its value chain, steering the portfolio towards lower‑carbon activities, and using climate scenario analysis to inform strategic and capital planning. The strategy connects value‑chain mapping, risk management, transition planning, climate‑related targets, resource allocation and scenario analysis.

Core elements include: a climate risk register with short‑, medium‑ and long‑term horizons; a value‑chain map covering own operations, suppliers and financing counterparties; summary climate targets for own operations and financed emissions; and a programme of climate‑related capital and operating expenditure. NGFS v4 climate scenarios are applied to the lending book to assess resilience under orderly, disorderly and hot house pathways, with modelled estimates of physical and transition risk losses, stranded assets and revenue at risk. These components are used together with portfolio metrics such as high‑carbon and fossil‑fuel exposure, financed emissions and carbon intensity to inform credit decisions, portfolio steering and resource allocation.

#### Sustainability risks and opportunities across the value chain

The 2024 climate risk register identifies eight material climate-related risks, comprising three physical and five transition risks, rated medium to high and mapped to short (0–2 years), medium (2–5 years) and long term (beyond 5 years) horizons. Key physical risks include high flood exposure in the mortgage portfolio and wildfire exposure in southern portfolios, both classified as acute physical risks with internal financial impact estimates of EUR 24.8 million and EUR 22.7 million respectively. Transition risks include technology‑driven shifts such as the move to electric vehicles in auto lending (high risk, short term, EUR 165.2 million estimated impact), policy‑driven carbon pricing on industrial loans (high risk, medium term, EUR 67.5 million), market‑driven stranded asset risk in fossil fuel sectors (high risk, long term, EUR 136.5 million), and reputational risk from scrutiny of financed emissions (medium risk, medium term, EUR 65.5 million).

Across the value chain, the most material concentrations arise downstream in the lending book. High‑carbon sector exposure represents 35.5% of the loan portfolio, or EUR 11,059.49 million, and fossil‑fuel exposure accounts for 22.15%, or EUR 6,899.59 million. The value‑chain map highlights significant exposures to corporate borrowers in the manufacture of coke and refined petroleum (EUR 3,903.65 million), mining of coal and lignite (EUR 3,547.58 million), mining of metal ores (EUR 2,749.58 million) and other mining and quarrying (EUR 2,102.44 million), all characterised by elevated transition policy risk from carbon pricing and demand shifts. Other material nodes include motor vehicle manufacturing and education, which are subject to moderate physical and transition risk.

Financed emissions from the lending portfolio are estimated at approximately 35.97 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, corresponding to a carbon intensity of about 1,155 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per million euro of exposure. These metrics are directly relevant to the identified reputational risk from financed‑emissions scrutiny and to the Bank’s financed‑emissions intensity reduction target, and they inform value‑chain prioritisation and portfolio steering in high‑carbon sectors.

Own operations, including banking buildings and the corporate fleet, contribute Scope 1 and 2 emissions and face chronic physical risks and transition market pressures, for example from energy efficiency requirements and evolving mobility patterns. Upstream suppliers are included in the value‑chain map, although quantified financial exposure is not available for all nodes.

Climate-related opportunities are identified across the value chain. The Bank has a green loan portfolio of EUR 3,335.75 million, representing 10.71% of total loans, and has identified five principal opportunity areas with an aggregate estimated revenue impact of EUR 465.0 million over varying horizons. These include expansion of green and sustainability‑linked lending aligned with the EU Taxonomy (estimated EUR 180.0 million revenue impact, medium term, medium confidence), growth in sustainable bond underwriting (EUR 95.0 million, short term, medium confidence), transition advisory services (EUR 42.0 million, medium term, medium confidence), renewable energy project finance (EUR 130.0 million, long term, medium confidence) and operational energy efficiency measures (EUR 18.0 million, short term, medium confidence). These figures represent estimates of potential revenue and are not assured outcomes.

#### Strategic management of climate-related risks and opportunities

The Bank’s strategic management of climate-related risks and opportunities is anchored in its climate‑related targets, portfolio steering mechanisms and integration of climate considerations into risk management and product development. Three summary targets guide the strategic direction: an absolute reduction target for Scope 1 and 2 emissions to 2030 that uses a 1.5°C pathway from the Science Based Targets initiative as a referenced framework and shows 40.0% progress in 2024; a financed‑emissions intensity reduction target for financed emissions to 2030 that uses the Net‑Zero Banking Alliance framework for target‑setting and shows 25.0% progress; and a net‑zero target across all scopes by 2050, also using the Net‑Zero Banking Alliance framework, with 13.3% progress. The available documentation does not specify whether these targets have been externally validated.

Transition risk management is supported by mechanisms such as climate scenario stress testing integrated into the internal capital adequacy assessment process for risks like the electric vehicle shift in auto lending; sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for industrial borrowers exposed to carbon pricing; and portfolio decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring to address reputational risk from financed emissions and to support progress against the financed‑emissions intensity target. For physical risks, the Bank applies collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlays in selected portfolios, and uses engagement and transition‑plan covenants with borrowers in flood‑exposed mortgage segments.

On the opportunity side, the strategy includes expanding green and sustainability‑linked lending, growing sustainable bond underwriting, and offering transition advisory and ESG due diligence services to corporate clients. Renewable energy project finance is positioned as a long‑term growth area in line with European energy transition policies. Operationally, energy efficiency upgrades and renewable energy certificate procurement are used to reduce Scope 2 emissions and operating costs.

The Bank defines short term as up to 2025, medium term as up to 2030 and long term as up to 2050, consistent with its strategic planning horizons and climate scenario analysis. While transition‑plan governance decisions and targets are in place, the detailed assumptions and dependencies underlying the transition plan are not specified in the Strategy evidence and are therefore not described further here.

#### Climate-related effects on business model, value chain and decision-making

Climate-related risks and opportunities are increasingly influencing Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s business focus, value‑chain priorities and decision‑making processes. The lending portfolio, with total loans of EUR 31,150.64 million, is being assessed through a climate lens, particularly in high‑carbon and fossil‑fuel sectors where exposure is significant. The identification of elevated transition risk in sectors such as fossil fuel extraction, refined petroleum manufacturing and carbon‑intensive mining, together with the portfolio’s financed emissions of approximately 35.97 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent and associated carbon intensity, is informing sectoral risk appetite, client selection and engagement priorities.

The value‑chain analysis highlights downstream financing counterparties as the primary channel through which climate risks and opportunities affect the business. This has led to greater emphasis on integrating climate risk factors, including financed‑emissions profiles, into credit analysis; using sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for high‑risk sectors; and developing products that support clients’ transition plans, such as sustainability‑linked loans and renewable energy project finance. Own operations are also being adjusted through energy efficiency initiatives and fleet considerations, although these are less material to the overall business model than lending activities.

Decision‑making at portfolio level is influenced by climate scenario outputs, which are used in capital planning and risk appetite discussions, and by progress against climate targets, which informs strategic priorities for green loan growth and financed‑emissions intensity reduction. The source data describes these portfolio and value‑chain effects but does not include a separate, detailed business‑model impact assessment or quantified analysis of strategic trade‑offs between climate objectives and other business goals.

#### Climate resilience and scenario analysis

Eurolux Universal Bank AG conducts climate scenario analysis using the NGFS v4 framework, assessing 18 scenarios that cover orderly, disorderly and hot house world pathways. The primary scope of analysis is the lending book across Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain, with sector‑level differentiation based on NACE classifications.

Methodologically, the orderly scenario analysis applies the NGFS v4 Net Zero 2050 pathway using a top‑down sector‑pathway approach. Carbon price assumptions are mapped from an international net‑zero emissions 2050 trajectory to counterparty sectors, and stranded asset estimates are derived from sector‑level fossil fuel capital expenditure exposure. Disorderly scenarios, such as Divergent Net Zero, incorporate high renewable energy shares but divergent sectoral transition speeds across jurisdictions, with carbon prices varying by country according to national emissions trading schemes and carbon tax trajectories. Hot house scenarios, including Nationally Determined Contributions and Current Policies, assume delayed or partial policy action, with carbon pricing ramp‑ups from 2030 onwards and higher temperature outcomes.

Key assumption ranges across the scenario set include carbon prices between EUR 1.2 and EUR 287.1 per tonne of CO2 equivalent, temperature outcomes between 1.5°C and 3.0°C, and technology readiness levels varying between low, medium and high depending on the scenario. Macroeconomic and energy system assumptions, such as growth rates and renewable energy shares, follow the NGFS v4 narratives, although detailed quantitative parameters are not reproduced in this Strategy section.

These figures are modelled estimates used for risk assessment and capital planning, not realised losses.

Resilience is assessed separately for each scenario type. Under orderly scenarios such as Net Zero 2050 and Below 2°C, transition risk losses remain within Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds, and mechanisms such as green loan growth, client engagement on transition plans and monitoring of financed‑emissions intensity are expected to reduce high‑carbon sector concentration over the medium term. Under disorderly scenarios, including Divergent Net Zero and Delayed Transition, near‑term resilience is assessed as acceptable, but medium‑term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is material; sector exposure limits and portfolio decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring are key mechanisms, and additional capital buffers may be required after 2030 under the most adverse assumptions. Under hot house scenarios, including Nationally Determined Contributions and Current Policies, physical risk losses become the dominant challenge by 2050, with collateral revaluation risk in flood‑exposed mortgage books and increased default risk for agricultural borrowers under chronic heat stress identified as primary transmission channels. - Operational Scope 1 and 2 absolute emissions reduction: 42% reduction by 2030 from a 2020 baseline of 137,849 tCO₂e, with interim target-record milestones for 2025 and 2028, using absolute tCO₂e as the progress metric, and with the SBTi 1.5°C pathway identified as the framework.
- Financed emissions intensity reduction: 30% reduction in tCO₂e per EUR million of lending by 2030 from a 2022 baseline of 20.0 tCO₂e per EUR million, with interim target-record milestones for 2025 and 2028, and with the Net‑Zero Banking Alliance identified as the framework.
- Net‑zero emissions: 100% reduction in net emissions across all scopes by 2050 from a 2020 baseline of 120,000 tCO₂e, with the Net‑Zero Banking Alliance identified as the framework and a recorded planned use of technology-based removal credits.

The target records also indicate review frequencies (triennial for the operational target, annual for the financed emissions intensity target and biennial for the net‑zero target). These review frequencies describe how often the Bank expects to revisit the target parameters. The target records include internal fields indicating that the targets are considered consistent with a temperature pathway; these fields are presented here as attributes of the target records and do not constitute an external assessment of pathway alignment.

The third‑party validation information described above is limited to the target records themselves. It does not extend to the Bank’s 2024 emissions metrics, to the reported progress percentages, or to any assessment of future target achievement. The Bank’s 2024 emissions and financial metrics, and the progress indicators reported against these targets, have not been independently verified.

Available documentation for this section does not describe which governance body oversees the setting of these targets, who the internal owners are, or whether and how related performance metrics are linked to remuneration. #### Metrics and targets limitations and evidence boundaries

The metrics and targets disclosed in this section are subject to the following limitations and evidence boundaries:

- Operational greenhouse gas emissions: detailed 2024 operational Scope 1, Scope 2 and Scope 3 emissions records are unavailable. Consequently, no quantified operational emissions are reported for 2024, and the operational emissions baseline and milestone values disclosed in the target descriptions are target-record parameters only and should not be interpreted as reported 2024 operational emissions.
- Financed emissions methodology: while 2024 financed emissions and portfolio carbon intensity figures are reported, detailed calculation methodology, data quality assessments, sector or asset-class coverage, and key assumptions are not described in the available documentation. Users of this report should therefore treat these figures as indicative estimates rather than fully specified, methodologically documented metrics.
- Exposure and green lending classifications: the criteria and taxonomies used to classify exposures as high‑carbon, fossil‑fuel or green, and to identify climate-related capital and operating expenditure, are not detailed in the available documentation. As a result, comparability with other institutions’ disclosures may be limited.
- Verification status: the 2024 metrics presented in this section, including financed emissions, carbon intensity, exposure metrics, green loans, and climate-related capital and operating expenditure, have not been independently verified. Progress percentages reported against the climate targets are internal target-record indicators and have not been independently verified.
- Target-record attributes: all baseline values, target values, interim milestones, progress percentages, framework references, review frequencies, and information on third‑party validation and planned use of carbon credits are taken from internal target records. These attributes describe how the Bank has defined its targets; they do not constitute verification of current-year emissions, confirmation of pathway alignment, or assurance that the targets will be met.
- Methodology and data quality: detailed information on the methods used to calculate the disclosed metrics, including data sources, estimation techniques, limitations and significant assumptions, is not provided in the available documentation. This may affect the ability of users to fully assess data quality and to compare the Bank’s metrics with those of other institutions or with future reporting periods.

Eurolux Universal Bank AG expects that, over time, enhancements to data availability, internal systems and methodologies will support more granular, comparable and methodologically transparent climate-related metrics and targets disclosures.
