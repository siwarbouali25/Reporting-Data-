### Strategy

#### Climate-related risks and opportunities

Eurolux Universal Bank AG identifies climate-related risks and opportunities using its risk register, portfolio metrics, targets, and climate scenario analysis.

Physical climate-related risks are primarily associated with acute and chronic weather-related impacts on financed assets and the bank’s own operations. The risk register highlights a high-rated physical acute risk related to the mortgage book’s flood exposure over the long-term (beyond 5 years), with an estimated financial impact of EUR 24.8 million. This risk is linked to climate scenario analysis (scenario SCN0004) and is being addressed through measures such as enhanced collateral assessment, including flood mapping overlays, and engagement with borrowers on transition and risk-mitigation covenants.

Transition climate-related risks are identified across market, policy, technology, and reputational dimensions:

- Transition technology risk in the auto lending portfolio is captured as a high-rated short-term risk (0–2 years) associated with the shift to electric vehicles. The risk register estimates a potential financial impact of EUR 165.2 million from changes in asset values, borrower credit quality, and product mix. This risk is linked to scenario analysis (scenario SCN0011) and is integrated into the bank’s Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) through climate scenario stress testing.

- Transition market risk related to potential stranded assets in fossil fuel sector exposures is identified as a high-rated long-term risk with an estimated financial impact of EUR 136.5 million. The bank has introduced collateral revaluation measures and applies flood mapping overlays as part of the broader risk response, recognising that stranded asset risk is strongly influenced by longer-term transition pathways.

- Transition policy risk from the introduction and escalation of carbon pricing affecting industrial loans is recorded as a high-rated medium-term risk (2–5 years), with estimated financial impact of EUR 67.5 million. Mitigation measures include sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for higher-emitting borrowers.

- Transition reputational risk connected to scrutiny of financed emissions is identified with a medium rating over a medium-term horizon and an estimated financial impact of EUR 65.5 million. The bank’s response focuses on monitoring a portfolio decarbonisation glide path and is linked to scenario analysis (scenario SCN0013).

The bank’s risk register and value chain mapping indicate that key transition-exposed sectors in the downstream financing value chain include manufacture of coke and refined petroleum (financial exposure of EUR 3,903.65 million), mining of coal and lignite (EUR 3,547.58 million), and mining of metal ores (EUR 2,749.58 million). These exposures are sensitive to transition policy and market risks such as carbon pricing, regulatory constraints, and demand shifts.

Chronic physical risks are identified for corporate borrowers in sectors such as manufacture of motor vehicles and education, which together represent several billion euros of exposure. These sectors face chronic climate-related disruptions such as heat stress and weather-related interruptions that may affect asset values and borrower creditworthiness.

Climate-related opportunities are not yet quantitatively captured as a separate opportunity register in the available evidence. However, Eurolux Universal Bank AG has a green loan portfolio of EUR 3,335.75 million, representing 10.71% of total loans, and is increasing climate-related capital and operating expenditure. While opportunity impacts are not quantified as a separate revenue line, these activities indicate potential for future climate-related revenue and margin uplift. In line with the stated boundary, any such opportunity impacts are considered estimates and are not presented as guaranteed revenue.

#### Effects on business model and value chain

Climate-related risks and transition considerations influence the bank’s business model primarily through its role as a universal lender and financier across multiple sectors and geographies. The available evidence indicates that the most material channels through which climate risks affect the bank’s business model are:

- Credit risk and collateral values in retail and mortgage lending, particularly in flood-exposed regions.
- Sectoral credit risk and stranded asset potential in carbon-intensive corporate lending.
- Reputational and regulatory pressure related to financed emissions in the corporate and institutional portfolios.
- Funding and capital implications from integrating climate risk into ICAAP and stress testing.

The value chain mapping identifies material nodes across own operations, upstream suppliers, and downstream financing counterparties:

- Own operations: The bank’s buildings are exposed to chronic physical risks such as heat and potential changes in cooling and maintenance needs. Corporate fleet and business travel are primarily exposed to transition market risks, including potential shifts in transportation policy, fuel pricing, and emission regulations.

- Downstream financing counterparties: The most material downstream nodes relate to corporate borrowers in:
  - Manufacture of coke and refined petroleum (EUR 3,903.65 million exposure).
  - Manufacture of motor vehicles (EUR 3,871.6 million exposure).
  - Mining of coal and lignite (EUR 3,547.58 million exposure).
  - Education (EUR 2,962.25 million exposure).
  - Mining of metal ores (EUR 2,749.58 million exposure).
  - Other mining and quarrying (EUR 2,102.44 million exposure).

These nodes are primarily associated with transition policy and market risks (such as carbon pricing, regulatory changes, and demand shifts) and chronic physical risks (such as heat and precipitation changes affecting facilities and infrastructure). All these examples are classified as downstream financing-counterparty nodes and fall within Scope 3 Category 15 (financed emissions).

The value chain boundary indicates that some own-operations and supplier nodes have been mapped qualitatively without full financial quantification. Accordingly, while the most material financing exposures are quantified, the total climate-related exposure across the entire value chain is not fully quantified at this stage.

The bank’s financed emissions amount to 35,973,167.69 tCO2e, with an overall carbon intensity of 1,154.81 tCO2e per EUR million. These metrics indicate that the bank’s business model, particularly its lending activities in fossil fuel-related sectors and high-emitting industries, is a significant channel through which climate-related transition risks and emerging opportunities materialise.

#### Effects on strategy and decision-making

Climate-related risks and opportunities influence Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s strategy and decision-making across several dimensions, guided by scenario analysis, risk assessments, targets, and capital allocation.

The bank has set climate-related targets, including:

- A Scope 1 and 2 absolute reduction target to 2030 under the SBTi 1.5°C framework (target TGT001).
- A Scope 3 Category 15 (financed emissions) intensity reduction target to 2030 under the Net-Zero Banking Alliance framework (target TGT002).
- A net-zero target across all scopes by 2050, also under the Net-Zero Banking Alliance (target TGT003).

All three targets are reported as “on track” based on the available evidence, and they influence portfolio steering, sector-level exposure management, and development of green lending products. While these frameworks are associated with 1.5°C and net-zero pathways, this report does not assert full Paris alignment; instead, it notes that these targets are designed to be consistent with commonly used 1.5°C-aligned frameworks, subject to further validation and implementation.

Scenario analysis based on NGFS v4 pathways is used to inform strategic decisions. Integration of climate scenario stress testing into the ICAAP indicates that climate risk outcomes are explicitly considered in capital planning, sector exposure limits, and risk appetite. For example, the high-rated risk linked to EV transition in auto lending is being addressed by adjusting underwriting standards, modifying residual value assumptions, and evaluating product strategies for internal combustion engine financing in the short term.
