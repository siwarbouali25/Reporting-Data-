### Strategy

#### Overview

Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s climate-related strategy is structured around identifying and managing material physical and transition risks across its value chain, steering the portfolio towards lower‑carbon activities, and using climate scenario analysis to inform strategic and capital planning. The strategy connects value‑chain mapping, risk management, transition planning, climate‑related targets, resource allocation and scenario analysis.

Core elements include: a climate risk register with short‑, medium‑ and long‑term horizons; a value‑chain map covering own operations, suppliers and financing counterparties; summary climate targets for own operations and financed emissions; and a programme of climate‑related capital and operating expenditure. NGFS v4 climate scenarios are applied to the lending book to assess resilience under orderly, disorderly and hot house pathways, with modelled estimates of physical and transition risk losses, stranded assets and revenue at risk. These components are used together with portfolio metrics such as high‑carbon and fossil‑fuel exposure, financed emissions and carbon intensity to inform credit decisions, portfolio steering and resource allocation.

#### Sustainability risks and opportunities across the value chain

The 2024 climate risk register identifies eight material climate-related risks, comprising three physical and five transition risks, rated medium to high and mapped to short (0–2 years), medium (2–5 years) and long term (beyond 5 years) horizons. Key physical risks include high flood exposure in the mortgage portfolio and wildfire exposure in southern portfolios, both classified as acute physical risks with internal financial impact estimates of EUR 24.8 million and EUR 22.7 million respectively. Transition risks include technology‑driven shifts such as the move to electric vehicles in auto lending (high risk, short term, EUR 165.2 million estimated impact), policy‑driven carbon pricing on industrial loans (high risk, medium term, EUR 67.5 million), market‑driven stranded asset risk in fossil fuel sectors (high risk, long term, EUR 136.5 million), and reputational risk from scrutiny of financed emissions (medium risk, medium term, EUR 65.5 million).

Across the value chain, the most material concentrations arise downstream in the lending book. High‑carbon sector exposure represents 35.5% of the loan portfolio, or EUR 11,059.49 million, and fossil‑fuel exposure accounts for 22.15%, or EUR 6,899.59 million. The value‑chain map highlights significant exposures to corporate borrowers in the manufacture of coke and refined petroleum (EUR 3,903.65 million), mining of coal and lignite (EUR 3,547.58 million), mining of metal ores (EUR 2,749.58 million) and other mining and quarrying (EUR 2,102.44 million), all characterised by elevated transition policy risk from carbon pricing and demand shifts. Other material nodes include motor vehicle manufacturing and education, which are subject to moderate physical and transition risk.

Financed emissions from the lending portfolio are estimated at approximately 35.97 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, corresponding to a carbon intensity of about 1,155 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per million euro of exposure. These metrics are directly relevant to the identified reputational risk from financed‑emissions scrutiny and to the Bank’s financed‑emissions intensity reduction target, and they inform value‑chain prioritisation and portfolio steering in high‑carbon sectors.

Own operations, including banking buildings and the corporate fleet, contribute Scope 1 and 2 emissions and face chronic physical risks and transition market pressures, for example from energy efficiency requirements and evolving mobility patterns. Upstream suppliers are included in the value‑chain map, although quantified financial exposure is not available for all nodes.

Climate-related opportunities are identified across the value chain. The Bank has a green loan portfolio of EUR 3,335.75 million, representing 10.71% of total loans, and has identified five principal opportunity areas with an aggregate estimated revenue impact of EUR 465.0 million over varying horizons. These include expansion of green and sustainability‑linked lending aligned with the EU Taxonomy (estimated EUR 180.0 million revenue impact, medium term, medium confidence), growth in sustainable bond underwriting (EUR 95.0 million, short term, medium confidence), transition advisory services (EUR 42.0 million, medium term, medium confidence), renewable energy project finance (EUR 130.0 million, long term, medium confidence) and operational energy efficiency measures (EUR 18.0 million, short term, medium confidence). These figures represent estimates of potential revenue and are not assured outcomes.

#### Strategic management of climate-related risks and opportunities

The Bank’s strategic management of climate-related risks and opportunities is anchored in its climate‑related targets, portfolio steering mechanisms and integration of climate considerations into risk management and product development. Three summary targets guide the strategic direction: an absolute reduction target for Scope 1 and 2 emissions to 2030 that uses a 1.5°C pathway from the Science Based Targets initiative as a referenced framework and shows 40.0% progress in 2024; a financed‑emissions intensity reduction target for financed emissions to 2030 that uses the Net‑Zero Banking Alliance framework for target‑setting and shows 25.0% progress; and a net‑zero target across all scopes by 2050, also using the Net‑Zero Banking Alliance framework, with 13.3% progress. The available documentation does not specify whether these targets have been externally validated.

Transition risk management is supported by mechanisms such as climate scenario stress testing integrated into the internal capital adequacy assessment process for risks like the electric vehicle shift in auto lending; sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for industrial borrowers exposed to carbon pricing; and portfolio decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring to address reputational risk from financed emissions and to support progress against the financed‑emissions intensity target. For physical risks, the Bank applies collateral revaluation and hazard mapping overlays in selected portfolios, and uses engagement and transition‑plan covenants with borrowers in flood‑exposed mortgage segments.

On the opportunity side, the strategy includes expanding green and sustainability‑linked lending, growing sustainable bond underwriting, and offering transition advisory and ESG due diligence services to corporate clients. Renewable energy project finance is positioned as a long‑term growth area in line with European energy transition policies. Operationally, energy efficiency upgrades and renewable energy certificate procurement are used to reduce Scope 2 emissions and operating costs.

The Bank defines short term as up to 2025, medium term as up to 2030 and long term as up to 2050, consistent with its strategic planning horizons and climate scenario analysis. While transition‑plan governance decisions and targets are in place, the detailed assumptions and dependencies underlying the transition plan are not specified in the Strategy evidence and are therefore not described further here.

#### Climate-related effects on business model, value chain and decision-making

Climate-related risks and opportunities are increasingly influencing Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s business focus, value‑chain priorities and decision‑making processes. The lending portfolio, with total loans of EUR 31,150.64 million, is being assessed through a climate lens, particularly in high‑carbon and fossil‑fuel sectors where exposure is significant. The identification of elevated transition risk in sectors such as fossil fuel extraction, refined petroleum manufacturing and carbon‑intensive mining, together with the portfolio’s financed emissions of approximately 35.97 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent and associated carbon intensity, is informing sectoral risk appetite, client selection and engagement priorities.

The value‑chain analysis highlights downstream financing counterparties as the primary channel through which climate risks and opportunities affect the business. This has led to greater emphasis on integrating climate risk factors, including financed‑emissions profiles, into credit analysis; using sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for high‑risk sectors; and developing products that support clients’ transition plans, such as sustainability‑linked loans and renewable energy project finance. Own operations are also being adjusted through energy efficiency initiatives and fleet considerations, although these are less material to the overall business model than lending activities.

Decision‑making at portfolio level is influenced by climate scenario outputs, which are used in capital planning and risk appetite discussions, and by progress against climate targets, which informs strategic priorities for green loan growth and financed‑emissions intensity reduction. The source data describes these portfolio and value‑chain effects but does not include a separate, detailed business‑model impact assessment or quantified analysis of strategic trade‑offs between climate objectives and other business goals.

#### Financial effects and resource allocation

Climate-related risks and opportunities have both current and anticipated financial effects for the Bank. For the reporting year 2024, climate-related capital expenditure amounts to EUR 476.95 million and climate-related operating expenditure to EUR 219.32 million. These figures indicate resource allocation towards climate-related initiatives, including portfolio transformation, risk management capabilities and operational efficiency measures. They are not presented as separate line items in the financial statements, and a detailed mapping to specific income statement or balance sheet captions is not available in the Strategy evidence.

High‑carbon sector exposure of EUR 11,059.49 million (35.5% of the loan portfolio) and fossil‑fuel exposure of EUR 6,899.59 million (22.15%) represent areas where transition risks could lead to credit losses, higher funding costs or capital requirements over the medium to long term. The climate risk register provides indicative financial impacts for selected risks, such as EUR 165.2 million for the electric vehicle transition in auto lending, EUR 136.5 million for stranded assets in fossil fuel sectors, and EUR 24.8 million and EUR 22.7 million for flood and wildfire physical risks respectively. These figures are internal estimates of potential impact rather than realised losses.

Financed emissions of approximately 35.97 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent and a carbon intensity of about 1,155 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per million euro of exposure are additional indicators of potential financial effects, particularly through reputational risk, evolving regulatory expectations and possible changes in capital requirements linked to high‑emitting exposures. These metrics are used alongside exposure measures in portfolio steering and in monitoring progress against the financed‑emissions intensity reduction target.

On the opportunity side, the Bank has identified an aggregate estimated revenue impact of EUR 465.0 million from climate-related business opportunities across green lending, sustainable bond underwriting, transition advisory, renewable energy project finance and operational energy efficiency. These are modelled estimates with medium confidence levels and should not be interpreted as assured future revenue.

Scenario analysis further informs anticipated financial effects. Modelled estimates indicate that, under specific NGFS v4 scenarios, physical risk losses could reach up to 32.0% of capital, transition risk losses up to 23.1% of capital, stranded assets up to EUR 33,575.2 million and revenue at risk up to EUR 35,509.1 million over various horizons. These estimates are used for strategic planning and capital adequacy assessment and do not represent actual losses incurred.

The Strategy evidence does not specify the Bank’s planned sources of funding to implement its climate strategy, such as the balance between retained earnings, debt issuance or other instruments.

#### Climate resilience and scenario analysis

Eurolux Universal Bank AG conducts climate scenario analysis using the NGFS v4 framework, assessing 18 scenarios that cover orderly, disorderly and hot house world pathways. The scenario set includes Net Zero 2050, Below 2°C, Divergent Net Zero, Delayed Transition, Nationally Determined Contributions and Current Policies scenarios, applied over short‑term (2025), medium‑term (2030) and long‑term (2050) horizons. The primary scope of analysis is the lending book across Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain, with sector‑level differentiation based on NACE classifications.

Methodologically, the orderly scenario analysis applies the NGFS v4 Net Zero 2050 pathway using a top‑down sector‑pathway approach. Carbon price assumptions are mapped from an international net‑zero emissions 2050 trajectory to counterparty sectors, and stranded asset estimates are derived from sector‑level fossil fuel capital expenditure exposure. Disorderly scenarios, such as Divergent Net Zero, incorporate high renewable energy shares but divergent sectoral transition speeds across jurisdictions, with carbon prices varying by country according to national emissions trading schemes and carbon tax trajectories. Hot house scenarios, including Nationally Determined Contributions and Current Policies, assume delayed or partial policy action, with carbon pricing ramp‑ups from 2030 onwards and higher temperature outcomes.

Key assumption ranges across the scenario set include carbon prices between EUR 1.2 and EUR 287.1 per tonne of CO2 equivalent, temperature outcomes between 1.5°C and 3.0°C, and technology readiness levels varying between low, medium and high depending on the scenario. Macroeconomic and energy system assumptions, such as growth rates and renewable energy shares, follow the NGFS v4 narratives, although detailed quantitative parameters are not reproduced in this Strategy section.

Modelled financial outputs from the scenario analysis include: a maximum physical risk loss of 32.0% of capital under the Current Policies hot house scenario in 2050; a maximum transition risk loss of 23.1% of capital under the Divergent Net Zero disorderly scenario in 2025; a maximum stranded assets estimate of EUR 33,575.2 million under the Net Zero 2050 orderly scenario in 2050, derived from fossil fuel sector capital expenditure exposure; and a maximum revenue at risk of EUR 35,509.1 million under the Nationally Determined Contributions hot house scenario in 2025. These figures are modelled estimates used for risk assessment and capital planning, not realised losses.

Resilience is assessed separately for each scenario type. Under orderly scenarios such as Net Zero 2050 and Below 2°C, transition risk losses remain within Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds, and mechanisms such as green loan growth, client engagement on transition plans and monitoring of financed‑emissions intensity are expected to reduce high‑carbon sector concentration over the medium term. Under disorderly scenarios, including Divergent Net Zero and Delayed Transition, near‑term resilience is assessed as acceptable, but medium‑term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is material; sector exposure limits and portfolio decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring are key mechanisms, and additional capital buffers may be required after 2030 under the most adverse assumptions. Under hot house scenarios, including Nationally Determined Contributions and Current Policies, physical risk losses become the dominant challenge by 2050, with collateral revaluation risk in flood‑exposed mortgage books and increased default risk for agricultural borrowers under chronic heat stress identified as primary transmission channels. Flood‑zone overlays and energy performance certificate‑based collateral monitoring are used as resilience mechanisms for these long‑term physical risks.

Adaptation capacity is considered through financial resources and portfolio flexibility. Climate-related capital expenditure of EUR 476.95 million and operating expenditure of EUR 219.32 million indicate a level of investment in climate risk management, data, systems and operational efficiency. Portfolio flexibility mechanisms include sector exposure limits, green loan growth (10.71% of the loan book), decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring, client engagement on transition plans and the use of financed‑emissions and carbon‑intensity metrics in portfolio steering. These mechanisms are intended to support the Bank’s ability to adjust its portfolio under different climate pathways, although the evidence does not provide enough detail to assess full operational adaptation capacity.

#### Strategy limitations and evidence boundaries

The Strategy disclosures for Eurolux Universal Bank AG are based on the source data described in the Strategy evidence set and are subject to several boundaries.

Business model: The source data describes climate-related risks, opportunities, portfolio metrics and value‑chain exposures, but does not include a separate, detailed business‑model impact assessment. As a result, the Strategy section focuses on identified risk and opportunity channels rather than a comprehensive redesign of the business model.

Trade‑offs: The available documentation does not provide quantified or documented trade‑off analysis for strategic decisions, for example between climate objectives and other financial or stakeholder considerations. Any references to strategic choices are therefore qualitative.

Targets: Only target summary information is available in this section. Accordingly, the Strategy disclosure is limited to target type, scope, status, target year, referenced framework and reported progress. Baseline details, milestones, validation bodies, gross or net status and planned carbon‑credit use are not described.

Transition plan: The evidence identifies transition‑related targets and governance decisions but does not specify the key assumptions or dependencies underlying the transition plan, such as policy trajectories, customer behaviour or technology deployment pathways. These dependencies are therefore not described in detail in this Strategy section.

Scenarios: All scenario‑related financial outputs, including physical and transition risk losses, stranded asset estimates and revenue at risk, are modelled estimates used for risk assessment and capital planning. They should not be interpreted as realised losses or as forecasts of actual future outcomes.

Opportunities: Revenue impacts associated with climate-related opportunities, including green lending, sustainable bond underwriting, transition advisory, renewable energy project finance and operational energy efficiency, are estimates with stated confidence levels and should not be interpreted as assured future revenue.

Resilience: Observations on resilience are specific to the NGFS v4 scenarios, assumptions and horizons described and do not constitute a general statement of overall bank‑wide resilience to all possible climate-related outcomes.
