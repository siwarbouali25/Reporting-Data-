### Risk management

#### Sustainability-related risk management overview

Eurolux Universal Bank AG integrates climate-related risks into its overall approach to risk management primarily through a structured climate risk register and the use of forward-looking climate scenario analysis as an input to risk assessment. For the year ended 31 December 2024, the climate risk register contains eight identified climate-related risks, comprising three physical risks and five transition risks. These risks are categorised into physical acute, physical chronic, transition market, transition policy, transition reputational and transition technology risk types, reflecting the different channels through which climate factors may affect the Bank’s credit, market and operational risk profiles.

Each risk in the register is characterised by a risk rating (high or medium), an assessed time horizon (short term: 0–2 years; medium term: 2–5 years; long term: more than 5 years), an estimate of potential financial impact expressed in millions of euro, and a defined monitoring frequency, which is either quarterly or semi-annual. The register also records where mitigation actions have been identified and whether individual risks are integrated into the Bank’s enterprise risk management processes. As at year-end 2024, six of the eight climate-related risks are flagged as integrated into enterprise risk management, representing 75% of the registered climate risks.

Selected risks are linked to scenario references drawn from the Bank’s climate scenario set. The Bank uses 18 climate-related scenarios, aligned with NGFS version 4, covering orderly, disorderly and hot-house world pathways, including scenarios such as Net Zero 2050, Below 2°C, Nationally Determined Contributions, Divergent Net Zero, Delayed Transition and Current Policies, with time horizons to 2025, 2030 and 2050. Scenario identifiers are associated with specific risks in the register and are used as forward-looking assessment inputs to test the sensitivity of those risks under different climate pathways; they are not treated as evidence of actual realised losses.

The risk register also records whether risks have changed since the prior period. In 2024, four of the eight climate-related risks are marked as changed compared with the preceding year, indicating refinements to risk definitions, assumptions or financial impact estimates in response to evolving climate and regulatory information. This change tracking supports ongoing reassessment of climate risk as part of the Bank’s broader risk oversight.

While governance structures overseeing risk management are described elsewhere in this report, the climate risk register and associated scenario references provide the primary mechanism through which climate-related risks are identified, assessed, prioritised, monitored and linked to the Bank’s wider risk management processes.

#### Upstream sustainability risks

For a commercial bank such as Eurolux Universal Bank AG, upstream sustainability-related risks would typically relate to dependencies on funding providers, key service suppliers and other external partners. The available documentation for 2024, however, is centred on the climate risk register and associated scenario analysis, which are focused on risks arising from the Bank’s financing activities, collateral exposures and borrower profiles rather than on upstream counterparties.

In the current reporting period, the climate risk register does not separately classify upstream climate risks or processes specific to suppliers, funding counterparties or other upstream entities. Any upstream exposure to climate-related disruption—for example, through market-wide funding conditions or service provider resilience—is therefore only indirectly reflected, where relevant, within the Bank’s broader transition market and policy risk categories. For instance, the registered risk of stranded assets related to fossil fuel sector exposure and the risk arising from carbon pricing on industrial loans capture climate transition dynamics that may also influence the Bank’s capital markets and funding environment, but these are recorded and managed primarily as credit and market risks linked to downstream lending and investment activities.

In the absence of a dedicated upstream climate risk taxonomy in the risk register, the Bank’s upstream sustainability risk management is currently best characterised as being embedded within its general risk considerations for funding and counterparty exposures, rather than as a distinct, climate-specific upstream process. Any future enhancements to explicitly identify and monitor upstream climate risks would be reflected through additional entries or categorisation within the climate risk register.

#### Risk management across internal operations

Internal operational climate risks for Eurolux Universal Bank AG may arise through potential disruption to branches, offices, data centres and critical processes resulting from extreme weather events or longer-term climatic changes. The climate risk register for 2024 captures physical risks that may have implications for internal operations, although these risks are primarily articulated through their impact on lending portfolios and collateral.

Two physical acute risks are registered: mortgage book flood exposure and wildfire exposure in the southern portfolio, along with a physical chronic risk relating to heat stress on agricultural borrowers. These risks are assessed with medium to high ratings and time horizons spanning short to long term, and they are monitored on quarterly or semi-annual cycles. While these entries are framed largely in terms of credit and collateral risk, the underlying hazard information—such as flood and wildfire exposure—also informs the Bank’s understanding of the potential for operational disruption in affected regions and may guide contingency planning for local operations.

The mitigation actions recorded in the risk register are primarily oriented towards financial risk management, including collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlay, and climate scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP for selected risks. These actions contribute indirectly to internal operational planning by improving the Bank’s understanding of climate hazard concentrations in its geographic footprint, although dedicated internal operational controls and procedures are not separately documented in the climate risk register.

On this basis, climate-related risk management across internal operations in 2024 is reflected through the alignment of physical risk assessment with the Bank’s geographic and collateral footprint, rather than through a standalone operational climate risk framework. Any further formalisation of operational climate controls, business continuity dependencies or critical service resilience would require additional documentation beyond the current register.

#### Downstream sustainability risks

Downstream sustainability-related risks are the primary channel through which climate factors affect Eurolux Universal Bank AG, given its role as a lender and financial intermediary. The 2024 climate risk register focuses on risks arising from lending portfolios, financed emissions, sectoral concentrations and the quality and location of collateral.

Physical risks are represented by mortgage book flood exposure, wildfire exposure in the southern portfolio and heat stress affecting agricultural borrowers. These risks are categorised as physical acute or physical chronic and are rated as high or medium. They are associated with specific time horizons—ranging from short term for wildfire and agricultural heat stress to long term for flood exposure—and are monitored quarterly or semi-annually. For example, the physical risk related to mortgage book flood exposure, rated high with a long-term horizon and an estimated potential financial impact of EUR 24.8 million, is monitored quarterly and is linked to a climate scenario reference. Mitigation actions recorded for these downstream exposures include collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlay, as well as engagement and transition-plan covenants with borrowers.

Transition risks in the downstream portfolio are prominent in the register and include:

- Technology-driven transition risk arising from the shift to electric vehicles in auto lending, rated high with a short-term horizon and an estimated potential financial impact of EUR 165.2 million, monitored quarterly and linked to climate scenario analysis.
- Market risk associated with stranded assets in the fossil fuel sector, rated high with a long-term horizon and an estimated potential financial impact of EUR 136.5 million, monitored quarterly.
- Policy-driven transition risks from carbon pricing on industrial loans and building energy performance regulation, with medium to high ratings and medium to long-term horizons.
- Reputational transition risk arising from scrutiny of financed emissions, rated medium with a medium-term horizon and an estimated potential financial impact of EUR 65.5 million, monitored semi-annually and linked to scenario analysis.

Mitigation actions for these downstream transition risks include climate scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP for selected risks, sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for high-emission activities, green retrofit financing incentives, portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring and collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlay for assets in higher-risk locations. These measures are recorded as risk responses in the risk register and are applied to specific risk categories and portfolios rather than uniformly across all lending activities.

In addition, the Bank’s reported climate-related metrics—such as total financed emissions of approximately 35.97 million tonnes of CO₂e, a portfolio carbon intensity of 1,154.8131 tonnes of CO₂e per million euro, high-carbon sector exposure of 35.5% and fossil fuel exposure of 22.15%—provide context for monitoring the scale and concentration of transition and reputational risks captured in the register. These indicators inform management’s understanding of exposure to carbon-intensive sectors; however, they do not constitute formal risk appetite thresholds in the available documentation.

Overall, downstream climate risk management during 2024 is characterised by the identification and monitoring of specific physical and transition risks within lending portfolios, the use of financial impact estimates and monitoring frequencies, and the recording of targeted mitigation actions within the risk register, with six of the eight risks flagged as integrated into the Bank’s enterprise risk management processes.

#### Climate risk management

Climate risk management at Eurolux Universal Bank AG during 2024 is structured around the climate risk register, which acts as the central repository for information on climate-related risks. The register distinguishes between physical and transition risks and records key attributes for each risk:

- Risk type and category: physical acute, physical chronic, transition market, transition policy, transition reputational and transition technology.
- Risk rating: high or medium, reflecting the relative assessed materiality.
- Time horizon: short term (0–2 years), medium term (2–5 years) and long term (more than 5 years).
- Estimated potential financial impact: expressed in millions of euro, providing a quantitative indication of potential effect on the Bank’s financial position over the relevant horizon.
- Monitoring frequency: specified as quarterly or semi-annual, indicating how often the risk is reviewed.
- Scenario analysis link: where applicable, a reference to one of the Bank’s climate scenarios.
- Mitigation actions: recorded management responses associated with each risk.
- Integration flag: indicating whether the risk is integrated into the Bank’s enterprise risk management processes.
- Change status: identifying whether the risk has changed since the prior period.

In 2024, the Bank identifies eight climate-related risks, with three physical and five transition risks. Four of these are recorded as having changed since the prior year, reflecting updated assessments or risk characterisations. The top risks by estimated financial impact are the transition technology risk linked to the electric vehicle shift in auto lending (EUR 165.2 million), transition market risk relating to stranded fossil fuel sector assets (EUR 136.5 million), transition policy risk from carbon pricing on industrial loans (EUR 67.5 million), transition reputational risk from financed emissions scrutiny (EUR 65.5 million) and physical acute risk from mortgage book flood exposure (EUR 24.8 million). These risks are monitored quarterly or semi-annually, consistent with their ratings and time horizons.

Scenario analysis is used as a forward-looking assessment input for selected risks in the register. Specific risks are linked to scenario identifiers such as SCN0002, SCN0004, SCN0011 and SCN0013, which correspond to scenarios within the Bank’s 18-scenario set, based on NGFS version 4 frameworks. These scenarios encompass orderly, disorderly and hot-house world pathways, including Net Zero 2050, Below 2°C, Nationally Determined Contributions, Divergent Net Zero, Delayed Transition and Current Policies, with analysis horizons to 2025, 2030 and 2050. The scenario references support the evaluation of how individual risks may evolve under different climate and policy trajectories and inform the design and prioritisation of mitigation actions. Scenario outputs are treated as modelled estimates to support risk assessment and are not presented as actual or forecast losses.

Mitigation actions recorded in the risk register illustrate how the Bank is managing identified climate-related risks. These actions include:

- Climate scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP for selected transition and physical risks.
- Collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlay for exposures in areas vulnerable to physical climate hazards.
- Engagement and transition-plan covenants with borrowers in relation to flood-exposed mortgage portfolios.
- Green retrofit financing incentives for properties affected by building energy performance regulation.
- Portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring to track progress against internal decarbonisation trajectories and to inform management of reputational and transition risks associated with financed emissions.
- Sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence for portfolios exposed to carbon pricing and high-emission sectors.

The enterprise risk management integration flag indicates that six out of eight climate-related risks are considered within the Bank’s broader risk management processes, representing 75% of the register. This integration manifests through linkages to capital planning (for example, scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP for selected risks) and through alignment with sector exposure management and due diligence practices recorded as mitigation actions. While the register does not detail the internal procedures or responsibilities for climate risk management, it provides quantified and time-bound information on the identified risks and mitigation actions that are operationally applied.

The climate risk management process has evolved since the prior period, as evidenced by the four risks marked as changed. This reflects an ongoing refinement of risk definitions, estimated financial impacts and mitigation approaches in response to developments in climate science, regulation and portfolio composition.

#### Risk management limitations and evidence boundaries

The climate risk management disclosures for Eurolux Universal Bank AG for 2024 are based primarily on the climate risk register, associated scenario references and selected climate-related key performance indicators. While this provides structured information on the identification, assessment, monitoring and management of climate-related risks, there are important limitations and evidence boundaries.

First, formal climate risk appetite thresholds, including quantitative limits or tolerances specific to climate-related risks, are not evidenced in the available documentation. Similarly, formal climate-specific risk policy documents, detailed control descriptions and procedural manuals, and defined escalation thresholds or triggers for climate-related risks are not available in the source data. As a result, this section does not describe formal risk appetite frameworks, detailed control activities or escalation routes for climate-related risks.

Second, climate-related risk management across the value chain is presented primarily through the lens of the climate risk register, which is focused on downstream lending, collateral and borrower-related risks. Upstream climate risk processes related to suppliers, funding providers or other external service partners are not separately documented, and internal operational climate controls are not detailed beyond the indirect implications of physical risk assessments.

Third, while selected risks are linked to specific climate scenario identifiers and the Bank uses a diverse set of 18 climate scenarios aligned to NGFS version 4, the disclosures do not include scenario-specific quantitative outcomes at portfolio level, nor do they describe how scenario outputs translate into binding risk limits. Scenario analysis is therefore presented as a qualitative and modelled input to risk assessment for individual registered risks, rather than as a comprehensive, quantitative description of climate resilience for all portfolios.

Finally, the reported climate-related metrics, including financed emissions, carbon intensity, high-carbon sector exposure and fossil fuel exposure, provide contextual information for understanding transition and reputational risks but are not evidenced as formal risk appetite limits or as being subject to external assurance in the context of this section. Users of this report should therefore interpret the risk management disclosures as reflecting the state of documented climate risk identification, assessment and mitigation processes as at 31 December 2024, subject to these evidence boundaries and to uncertainty inherent in forward-looking climate scenario analysis and financial impact estimation.
