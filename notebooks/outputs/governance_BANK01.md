### Governance

#### Board oversight

In 2024, the supervisory board of Eurolux Universal Bank AG comprised 10 members, of whom 68.5% were independent. Climate-related matters were overseen by the Full Board and supported by a dedicated ESG & Sustainability Committee. The available evidence demonstrates that climate topics formed a recurring part of the board’s work: climate-related items appeared on the Full Board agenda in 72.6% of meetings in 2024, compared with 69.8% in 2022 and 73.1% in 2023. The Full Board met 8 times in 2024 (4 times in 2022 and 7 times in 2023).

The ESG & Sustainability Committee met 7 times in 2024 (5 times in 2022 and 6 times in 2023), providing a focused forum for climate and broader ESG topics. Available documentation evidences committee activity and meeting frequency but does not include a committee charter or terms of reference; as a result, a formal written climate mandate for the committee is not described.

The Full Board receives semi‑annual climate risk reports, according to the 2024 governance data. These reports support the board’s oversight of climate-related risks and opportunities across Eurolux Universal Bank AG’s activities. In 2024, the board’s climate oversight also covered major strategic topics, including transition planning and net‑zero targets. Climate considerations were included in the review of major transactions, as evidenced by the 2024 indicator that major transactions are subject to climate checks. The available evidence does not provide further detail on the formalisation of these checks as a policy or mandatory control.

Board-level decision-making on climate in 2024 included: (i) approval of climate-related public reporting, (ii) endorsement of the bank’s transition plan, and (iii) endorsement of revisions to net-zero interim targets. These decisions are described in more detail in the “Board and committee decisions during 2024” subsection. The available board decision evidence identifies the climate-related decisions and the responsible body (Full Board or ESG & Sustainability Committee) but does not describe specific trade‑offs considered, such as profitability, capital allocation, implementation cost, risk appetite or competing strategic priorities.

#### Management responsibility

Management responsibility for climate-related matters in 2024 was coordinated through the Climate Risk Management Committee. Comparative governance data show that different management committee names were recorded in prior years (“Group Sustainability Committee” in 2022 and “ESG Executive Committee” in 2023). The available evidence does not indicate whether these committees are organisationally linked.

Climate-related risk management followed a structured process underpinned by a dedicated climate risk register:

- Identification: In 2024, management maintained a climate risk register that recorded 8 climate-related risks. Of these, 6 risks were flagged as integrated into the enterprise risk management (ERM) framework. The register covered both physical and transition risks, including categories such as physical acute, physical chronic, transition market, transition policy, transition reputational and transition technology risks.

- Classification: Each risk in the register was classified by category, time horizon and rating. Ratings in 2024 included “high” and “medium”. Time horizons included short (0–2 years), medium (2–5 years) and long (5+ years). For example, “Transition risk — EV shift in auto lending” (risk ID RISK-BANK01-2024-0006) was classified as a high transition technology risk with a short (0–2 year) horizon, while “Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure” (risk ID RISK-BANK01-2024-0001) was classified as a high physical acute risk with a long (5+ year) horizon.

- Monitoring: The register specified monitoring frequencies for each risk, using quarterly or semi‑annual cycles. In 2024, many high‑rated risks (for example, RISK-BANK01-2024-0006, RISK-BANK01-2024-0005, RISK-BANK01-2024-0008 and RISK-BANK01-2024-0001) were monitored quarterly, while some medium risks such as “Reputational risk — financed emissions scrutiny” (risk ID RISK-BANK01-2024-0007) were monitored on a semi‑annual basis.

- Scenario analysis: Where relevant, risks were linked to climate scenario analyses. In 2024, the risk register included explicit scenario references (e.g. SCN0002, SCN0004, SCN0011, SCN0013). For individual risks, “Transition risk — EV shift in auto lending” was linked to scenario SCN0011, “Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure” to SCN0004 and “Reputational risk — financed emissions scrutiny” to SCN0013. Not all high‑rated risks had an associated scenario link; for example, “Stranded assets — fossil fuel sector exposure” and “Transition risk — carbon pricing on industrial loans” were recorded without a scenario reference.

- Mitigation: For each registered risk, management documented one or more mitigation actions. In 2024, these included:
  - climate scenario stress testing integrated into the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP);
  - collateral revaluation and flood mapping overlays;
  - engagement and transition‑plan covenants with borrowers;
  - green retrofit financing incentives;
  - portfolio decarbonisation glide‑path monitoring; and
  - sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence.

  For example, “Transition risk — EV shift in auto lending” is mitigated through climate scenario stress testing integrated into ICAAP, and “Transition risk — carbon pricing on industrial loans” is mitigated via sector exposure limits and enhanced due diligence.

- ERM integration and reporting: In 2024, 6 of the 8 climate risks were recorded as integrated into ERM. This integration supports the incorporation of climate-related risks into broader risk reporting and management processes. The climate risk register, combined with ERM integration and the stated monitoring frequencies (quarterly and semi‑annual), supports management monitoring of climate-related exposures and enables board or committee review where required. The available evidence does not describe formal escalation thresholds or routes for moving climate risks from management to board level.

The risk register was updated during 2024, with 4 risks recorded as having changed compared with the prior period. The available evidence does not provide additional detail on the nature of these changes.

#### Climate skills and competencies

In 2024, 37.5% of the board members of Eurolux Universal Bank AG were reported to have climate-related expertise, up from 33.5% in 2022 and 35.5% in 2023. These percentages reflect an outcome metric on the prevalence of climate-related skills and experience at board level.

Management operates a climate-related skills development programme in 2024. The available evidence confirms the existence of this programme but does not provide detail on its content, frequency or participation rates. The combination of the reported board climate expertise percentage and the presence of a skills development programme indicates that climate-related competencies are being developed and maintained among governance bodies.

The available evidence does not describe a formal process for assessing the adequacy of climate-related skills at board or management level (for example, through structured skills matrices, periodic evaluations or defined competency thresholds). As a result, no formal skills adequacy assessment process is reported.

#### Remuneration and climate incentives

Climate-related performance is integrated into executive remuneration at Eurolux Universal Bank AG. In 2024, the chief executive officer’s compensation included an ESG‑linked component of 9.3%, compared with 6.3% in 2022 and 7.8% in 2023. For the wider executive population, 15.1% of remuneration was linked to climate-related measures in 2024, compared with 8.7% in 2022 and 15.3% in 2023.

The available evidence confirms that the CEO’s compensation is ESG‑linked and provides the percentage figures cited above, but it does not describe the specific climate metrics, targets or weightings used within the ESG components. Similarly, for all executives, the data provide the proportion of remuneration linked to climate-related measures but do not detail the underlying performance indicators or whether they are tied to risk metrics, emissions, green lending volumes or other outcomes.

#### Board and committee decisions during 2024

In 2024, the board and its ESG & Sustainability Committee took several notable climate-related decisions:

- On 24 January 2024, the Full Board approved the 2024 ESG report for publication. Topics discussed at this meeting included transition plan review and carbon credit approval.

- On 15 April 2024, the ESG & Sustainability Committee approved the climate scenario analysis methodology, following discussion of TCFD-related matters and a physical risk update.

- On the same date, 15 April 2024, the ESG & Sustainability Committee also approved a carbon credit procurement budget, following discussions covering physical risk updates, TCFD review and transition plan review.

- On 7 October 2024, the Full Board endorsed an updated transition plan, after considering topics related to TCFD review and green finance growth.

- On 1 November 2024, the Full Board endorsed a revision to the bank’s net‑zero interim targets, following discussion of progress towards net zero.

These decisions illustrate how climate-related topics were addressed at both board and committee levels during the reporting year, including methodological foundations for scenario analysis, resource allocation for carbon credit procurement, and strategic direction for transition planning and net‑zero target setting.

#### External assurance and controls

In 2024, Eurolux Universal Bank AG obtained external limited assurance over specific elements of its climate-related information. EY provided limited assurance in accordance with ISAE 3000 over the bank’s Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions. Limited assurance represents a lower level of assurance than reasonable assurance and involves procedures that are primarily inquiry and analytical review, rather than the more extensive testing associated with reasonable assurance engagements.

The assurance scope, as evidenced, is restricted to Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions. For a bank, this means that financed emissions and other Scope 3 categories are outside the stated assurance scope based on the available evidence. No external assurance is reported for climate-related governance processes, climate risk registers, scenario analyses, transition plans or net‑zero targets.

The combination of externally assured Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions and internally managed governance and risk processes provides a control environment in which certain emissions data points are subject to independent verification, while the broader climate governance framework is overseen by the board and management without external assurance described in the available documentation.
