## General Requirements

### Purpose and basis of preparation
This section explains the general requirements applied in preparing the sustainability-related financial disclosures for the reporting year 2024. The disclosures are prepared to present fairly sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the bank’s prospects, including effects on cash flows, access to finance and cost of capital over the short, medium and long term. Fair presentation is supported through a complete, neutral and accurate depiction of the sustainability-related risks and opportunities addressed in this report, and through information that is comparable, verifiable, timely and understandable.

The disclosures are structured to enable users to understand connections (i) between sustainability-related risks and opportunities and (ii) between disclosures across governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets. For example, climate-related risks identified and monitored through the climate risk register are connected to governance oversight (including climate risk reporting to the board) and to strategy analysis through climate scenario resilience assessments.

### Reporting period, fiscal year-end and comparatives
The reporting year is **2024**, with a fiscal year-end of **31 December 2024**. Comparative information is presented for **2022** and **2023** where available and relevant to understanding performance and trends.

Comparatives are used to support trend analysis in metrics that are tracked over multiple years. For example, climate-related capital expenditure is presented for 2022–2024 (2022: **€322.2 million**; 2023: **€373.91 million**; 2024: **€476.95 million**), and Tier 1 capital is presented for 2022–2024 (2022: **€95,149.4 million**; 2023: **€143,979.2 million**; 2024: **€151,208.6 million**). Scope 1 emissions are also tracked across 2022, 2023 and 2024.

### Reporting currency
The reporting currency is **EUR**. Where currency is used as a unit of measure in these sustainability-related financial disclosures (for example, climate-related capital expenditure and Tier 1 capital), amounts are presented in euros.

### Connected information across the disclosures
The disclosures are prepared to provide connected information that helps users understand how climate-related risks and opportunities are identified, assessed, monitored and managed, and how these matters relate to performance.

**Connections between risks and opportunities.** The climate risk register includes risks spanning acute physical risk, transition risk and reputational risk. Examples of risk descriptions included in the register are:

- **Acute physical risk (mortgage collateral flood exposure):** residential and commercial mortgage collateral located in flood-prone zones faces rising acute physical risk; property damage and devaluation could impair collateral coverage.
- **Chronic physical risk (agricultural counterparties):** chronic heat and shifting precipitation reduce yields for agricultural counterparties, weakening repayment capacity over the medium term.
- **Transition risk (electrification):** rapid electrification could strand internal-combustion vehicle manufacturers and suppliers in the lending book.
- **Reputational risk (stakeholder scrutiny):** stakeholder and NGO scrutiny of financed emissions and fossil financing could damage brand and trigger client attrition.
- **Policy and standards risk (energy performance):** minimum energy performance standards may require costly retrofits for low-EPC mortgage collateral, reducing values and increasing loan-to-value.

These risks are connected to the bank’s financed emissions profile and portfolio resilience analysis. For example, stakeholder scrutiny of financed emissions is linked to the bank’s financed emissions metric for 2024 (**35,973,167.6878965 tCO₂e**) and to the way the bank assesses transition pathways through scenario analysis.

**Connections across governance, risk management and strategy.** Governance oversight and risk monitoring are connected through defined reporting and monitoring frequencies. Climate risk reporting to the board occurs **semi-annually**, and an ESG committee exists with **5 meetings per year**. The board has a size of **10**, with **33.5%** board climate expertise, and climate topics represent **69.8%** of the board agenda. Management-level governance structures referenced in the disclosures include the **Group Sustainability Committee**, **ESG Executive Committee**, and **Climate Risk Management Committee**.

Risk monitoring is operationalised through the climate risk register, which includes monitoring frequencies such as **monthly**, **quarterly**, and **semi-annual**. For example, the “Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure” risk is monitored **monthly**.

Strategy and resilience are connected through climate scenario analysis. Scenario methodology notes describe the use of NGFS v4 scenarios applied to the banking book, including:

- **NGFS v4 Net Zero 2050:** applied using a top-down sector-pathway approach; carbon price assumptions from the IEA NZE 2050 trajectory are mapped to counterparty NACE sectors; stranded asset estimates are derived from sector-level fossil fuel capex exposure; scope covers all lending book exposures across **DE, FR, IT, NL and ES** jurisdictions.
- **NGFS v4 Below 2°C (orderly):** applied using NGFS integrated assessment model outputs aligned to a **1.8°C** median temperature pathway; carbon price trajectory from the REMIND-MAgPIE model; financial impact assessed through loan-level probability of default sensitivity to carbon cost pass-through by sector carbon intensity tier.

Resilience assessments connect scenario outcomes to capital considerations and portfolio management mechanisms. Under orderly transition scenarios, the portfolio is assessed as demonstrating adequate resilience, with transition risk losses remaining within Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds. Under disorderly transition scenarios, near-term resilience is assessed as acceptable, while medium-term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is assessed as material; sector exposure limits and portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring are described as primary resilience mechanisms, and additional capital buffers may be required post-2030 under the most adverse disorderly assumptions.

### Methodologies and measurement approaches
This report uses defined methodologies for key climate-related measures and risk assessments.

**Risk rating methodology (risk register).** Climate risk ratings are derived from a **5×5 risk matrix** based on the product of a likelihood score and a severity score. The resulting score is mapped to rating bands as follows:

- **Low:** scores below 3
- **Medium:** scores from 3 to 6
- **High:** scores from 8 to 12
- **Critical:** scores from 15 to 25

This methodology is applied to individual risks in the climate risk register. For example, “Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure” has a likelihood score of **4** and a severity score of **5**, resulting in a score of **20** and a **critical** risk rating.

**Scenario analysis methodology.** Scenario analysis is performed using NGFS v4 scenarios and specified modelling approaches. For the Net Zero 2050 scenario, the bank applies a top-down sector-pathway approach, mapping carbon price assumptions from the IEA NZE 2050 trajectory to counterparty NACE sectors and deriving stranded asset estimates from sector-level fossil fuel capex exposure. For the Below 2°C (orderly) scenario, the bank uses NGFS integrated assessment model outputs aligned to a 1.8°C median temperature pathway, with carbon prices from the REMIND-MAgPIE model, and assesses financial impact through loan-level probability of default sensitivity to carbon cost pass-through by sector carbon intensity tier.

**Market-based Scope 2 accounting and renewable instruments.** Scope 2 market-based emissions are sourced directly from utility invoice data that already reflects market-based accounting with grid emission factors adjusted for renewable energy certificates and power purchase agreements. Renewable certificate registry data is used for disclosure narrative (certificate type, volume and registry) and is not subtracted again during preparation, avoiding double-counting.

**Financed emissions methodology note (sovereign bonds).** For sovereign bonds, issuer EVIC and issuer revenue are treated as not applicable because EVIC and revenue are corporate concepts and are not used for sovereigns.

### Estimates, assumptions and judgement
Sustainability-related financial disclosures necessarily involve estimates and judgement, particularly where forward-looking analysis is required.

- **Scenario analysis** incorporates assumptions about carbon price trajectories (including those from the IEA NZE 2050 trajectory and the REMIND-MAgPIE model) and uses sector mapping to counterparty NACE sectors. The assessment of resilience under orderly and disorderly transition scenarios reflects judgement about how transition pathways translate into credit risk and stranded asset impairments.
- **Risk assessment** involves judgement in assigning likelihood and severity scores within the 5×5 matrix. The resulting rating bands provide a consistent basis for prioritisation and monitoring.

These estimates and judgements are connected to governance and risk management through defined monitoring and reporting cadences (for example, monthly monitoring for selected risks and semi-annual reporting of climate risk to the board).

### Data quality characteristics and assurance
The disclosures are prepared to support faithful representation and decision-usefulness, including through transparency about data quality characteristics.

For emissions-related information, the bank summarises the composition of emissions data quality inputs as follows:

- **Estimated (economic): 47.5%**
- **CDP disclosure: 35.5%**
- **Proxy model: 16.3%**
- **Audited report: 0.7%**

In addition, external assurance over sustainability information is described as **limited**.

### Consistency, comparability and change monitoring
Comparability is supported through the use of consistent reporting periods (2024 with comparatives for 2022 and 2023) and consistent measurement approaches for key metrics.

Change monitoring is embedded in the climate risk register through an indicator of whether a risk has changed since the prior period, alongside defined monitoring frequencies. For example, the “Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure” entry indicates it has **not changed since the prior period** and is monitored **monthly**, while the stakeholder scrutiny risk indicates it **has changed since the prior period** and is monitored **quarterly**.

Where metrics are presented for multiple years, they are intended to support users’ understanding of trends relevant to prospects. For example, climate-related capital expenditure increased from **€322.2 million** (2022) to **€373.91 million** (2023) and **€476.95 million** (2024), and Tier 1 capital increased from **€95,149.4 million** (2022) to **€143,979.2 million** (2023) and **€151,208.6 million** (2024). These metrics provide context for understanding the bank’s capacity to manage climate-related risks and opportunities alongside the resilience assessments described in scenario analysis.

### Value chain boundary considerations (connectivity)
The bank’s value chain mapping identifies material nodes within its own operations, including **“Corporate fleet & business travel”**. This is connected to operational emissions disclosures, including Scope 1 emissions (with fleet included) and Scope 3 travel emissions for 2024 (**289.8833 tCO₂e**), supporting a coherent view of operational sources alongside financed emissions and portfolio-related climate risks.
