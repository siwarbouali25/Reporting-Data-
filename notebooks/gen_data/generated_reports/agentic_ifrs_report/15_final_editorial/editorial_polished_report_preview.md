# IFRS S1/S2 Sustainability-Related Financial Disclosures

# 1. General Requirements

This section explains the basis on which the Bank’s sustainability-related financial disclosures have been prepared, including the reporting period and presentation currency, the approach to comparative information, and how the Bank presents connected information. It also summarises key methodologies, estimates and judgements applied to climate-related disclosures.

### Basis of preparation and fair presentation

The Bank prepares its sustainability-related financial disclosures to present fairly sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the Bank’s prospects. Fair presentation is supported through disclosures that are intended to be complete, neutral and accurate, and that are prepared in a manner designed to be comparable, verifiable, timely and understandable.

The disclosures are organised to help users understand:

- **connections between sustainability-related risks and opportunities** that could reasonably be expected to affect the Bank’s prospects; and
- **connections across the disclosure pillars** (governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets), including how oversight and monitoring relate to the measurement of performance.

Where quantitative measures are presented, the Bank uses consistent units of measure and provides accompanying methodology notes to support interpretability.

### Reporting period and reporting currency

These sustainability-related financial disclosures are prepared for the reporting year **2024**, with a fiscal year end of **31 December 2024**.

The presentation currency used in these sustainability-related financial disclosures is **euro (EUR)**. Where currency is used as a unit of measure (for example, climate-related capital expenditure and Tier 1 capital), amounts are presented in **EUR millions**.

### Connected information

The Bank prepares its sustainability-related financial disclosures to enable users to understand connections (i) between related sustainability matters and (ii) across the disclosures provided.

**Connections across governance, risk management and metrics.** Climate-related risks are monitored at defined frequencies and are reported to the Board on a **semi-annual** basis. Governance indicators (including Board climate expertise and the frequency of Board and committee meetings) are presented alongside climate-related metrics (including financed emissions, operational emissions and climate-related capital expenditure) to support an integrated understanding of how oversight and monitoring relate to performance measurement.

**Connections across risk identification, assessment and monitoring.** The climate risk register includes risk descriptions, risk categories, risk ratings, and monitoring frequencies. For example:

- **Reputational risk (example):** stakeholder and NGO scrutiny of financed emissions and fossil financing could damage brand and trigger client attrition.

**Connections between scenario analysis and resilience assessment.** The Bank uses climate scenario analysis to assess portfolio resilience under orderly and disorderly transition pathways. Methodology notes describe scenario sources and modelling approaches, and resilience assessments describe implications for portfolio resilience and capital consumption.

### Methodologies, estimates and judgements

This subsection summarises principal methodologies used in preparing climate-related disclosures, focusing on scenario analysis, climate risk rating, and greenhouse gas accounting approaches that underpin reported metrics.

#### Climate scenario analysis methodology

The Bank applies climate scenarios to the banking book using methodologies that link scenario pathways to sector exposures.

- **NGFS v4 Net Zero 2050 scenario (orderly transition).** The scenario is applied using a top-down sector-pathway approach. Carbon price assumptions are taken from the IEA Net Zero Emissions by 2050 trajectory and mapped to counterparty NACE sectors. Stranded asset estimates are derived from sector-level fossil fuel capital expenditure exposure. The analysis scope covers all lending book exposures across **Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain**.

Resilience assessments are presented for both orderly and disorderly transition scenarios:

- Under **orderly transition scenarios**, the portfolio demonstrates adequate resilience. Transition risk losses remain within Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds. Green loan growth and client engagement on transition plans are expected to progressively reduce high-carbon sector concentration over the medium term.
- Under **disorderly transition scenarios**, near-term resilience is acceptable but medium-term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is material. The Bank’s current sector exposure limits and active portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring are the primary resilience mechanisms. Additional capital buffers may be required post-2030 under the most adverse disorderly assumptions.

These assessments are forward-looking and involve judgement in selecting scenario pathways and translating scenario variables (including carbon prices) into sector and counterparty impacts.

#### Climate risk rating methodology

Climate risk ratings in the risk register are derived from a **5×5 risk matrix** based on likelihood and severity scores. The overall score is calculated as the product of the likelihood score and the severity score.

Risk rating bands are defined as follows:

- **Low:** score less than 3
- **Medium:** score from 3 to 6
- **High:** score from 8 to 12
- **Critical:** score from 15 to 25

As an illustration of application, “Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure” is rated **critical** with a likelihood score of **4** and a severity score of **5** (score 20).

#### Greenhouse gas emissions accounting and Scope 2 market-based approach

Operational emissions metrics are presented for 2024, including Scope 1, Scope 2 (location-based and market-based), and Scope 3 business travel.

For **Scope 2 market-based emissions**, the Bank’s market-based figure is sourced directly from utility invoice data that already reflects market-based accounting. Grid emission factors are adjusted for renewable energy certificates and power purchase agreements. Renewable energy certificate registry information is used for disclosure narrative (for example, certificate type, volume and registry) and is not subtracted again in the calculation, avoiding double-counting.

#### Financed emissions and intensity metrics

The Bank reports financed emissions for 2024 and a carbon intensity metric expressed as tonnes of CO₂e per EUR million.

For sovereign bonds, issuer enterprise value including cash and issuer revenue are not used because these are corporate concepts not applicable to sovereigns.

# 2. Governance

This section describes how governance arrangements support oversight of sustainability-related risks and opportunities, with a focus on climate-related risks and opportunities.

The Board provides oversight of climate-related risks and opportunities through its regular governance processes. The Board comprises **10 directors** and held **4 full Board meetings** during the period.

- Review of climate-related disclosure frameworks (including **TCFD review**);

To support effective oversight, the Board receives climate risk reporting on a **semi-annual** basis. This reporting cadence is designed to ensure the Board is informed about climate-related risks and opportunities with sufficient regularity to oversee strategy and risk management.

# 3. Strategy

This section explains how climate-related risks and opportunities are expected to affect the bank’s business model, strategy and financial planning across the **short term**, **medium term** and **long term**, and how the bank assesses the resilience of its strategy under climate-related scenario analysis.

### Climate-related risks and opportunities and their expected effects

The bank’s climate-related risk profile is driven primarily by exposures in the banking book and the sensitivity of collateral and counterparties to both **physical** and **transition** risk transmission channels.

#### Principal climate-related risks

The bank identifies the following climate-related risks as relevant to its strategy:

- **Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure**: Residential and commercial mortgage collateral located in flood-prone zones faces rising acute physical risk. Property damage and devaluation could impair collateral coverage.
- **Physical risk — wildfire exposure in southern portfolio** (risk rating: **medium**): Wildfire risk is assessed as a physical risk driver for parts of the portfolio.
- **Transition risk — building energy performance regulation** (risk rating: **medium**): Minimum energy performance standards may require costly retrofits for low-EPC mortgage collateral, reducing values and increasing loan-to-value.
- **Stranded assets — fossil fuel sector exposure**: Demand shifts away from fossil fuels risk stranding reserves and assets held by oil, gas and coal counterparties, reducing their enterprise value.
- **Carbon pricing and policy tightening**: Tightening EU ETS and national carbon pricing raise operating costs for carbon-intensive borrowers, increasing default probability in cement, steel and chemicals.
- **Technology transition risk in automotive value chains**: Rapid electrification could strand internal-combustion vehicle manufacturers and suppliers in the lending book.
- **Physical risk affecting agriculture (medium term)**: Chronic heat and shifting precipitation reduce yields for agricultural counterparties, weakening repayment capacity over the medium term.
- **Reputational and stakeholder risk**: Stakeholder and NGO scrutiny of financed emissions and fossil financing could damage brand and trigger client attrition.

These risks can affect the bank through credit risk (counterparty repayment capacity), collateral value dynamics (including mortgage collateral revaluation), and broader franchise impacts (including reputational effects and client behaviour).

#### Principal climate-related opportunities

The bank identifies climate-related opportunities that are aligned with client demand and the transition of the real economy:

- **Sustainable bond underwriting (short term)**: Growth in underwriting activity for sustainable bonds.
- **Green loan growth (medium term)**: Expansion of green lending.
- **Transition advisory**: Fee income from climate risk advisory, transition planning support, and ESG due diligence services offered to large corporate clients.
- **Renewable energy project finance**: Project finance for solar, wind, and battery storage infrastructure aligned with REPowerEU and national renewable energy targets.

These opportunities are expected to support revenue diversification and deepen client relationships as counterparties seek financing and advisory support for transition plans and low-carbon investment.

### Effects on the value chain

This subsection explains where climate-related risks and opportunities arise across the bank’s value chain, with emphasis on the banking model in which the most material climate-related effects typically occur through financing activities.

- **Own operations**: Operational sites and travel generate Scope 1–2 emissions and face physical disruption. The bank’s value chain mapping also identifies **Corporate fleet & business travel** as an own-operations node with the same climate risk description.
- **Financing counterparties**: Climate-related risks are concentrated in financed activities, including:
  - Lending to **Manufacture of coke and refined petroleum**, which carries elevated transition risk from carbon pricing and demand shifts.
  - Exposure to **Manufacture of motor vehicles**, which is subject to moderate physical and transition risk.

The bank’s strategy therefore focuses on managing climate-related risk in the banking book while pursuing opportunities that support clients’ transition and the financing of renewable energy infrastructure.

### Strategic response and integration into decision-making

The bank’s strategic response is oriented around (i) managing climate-related risk concentrations and (ii) capturing transition-related business opportunities.

#### Portfolio and client strategy

Across the banking book, the bank’s strategic response emphasises:

- **Sector and portfolio steering**: Scenario resilience assessments identify the bank’s **sector exposure limits** and **active portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring** as primary mechanisms supporting resilience under disorderly transition scenarios.
- **Client engagement**: Under orderly transition scenarios, the bank expects **green loan growth** and **client engagement on transition plans** to progressively reduce high-carbon sector concentration over the medium term.
- **Collateral-focused risk management**: For physical and building-related transition risks, resilience mechanisms referenced in scenario analysis include a **flood-zone overlay** and **EPC-based collateral monitoring**, reflecting the importance of location and energy performance characteristics in mortgage collateral risk.

#### Emissions and intensity orientation

The bank discloses a target intensity of **20.0 tCO₂e per €m**. In addition, the bank reports **financed emissions of 35,973,167.7 tCO₂e (2024)**, reflecting the strategic relevance of financed emissions to the bank’s climate-related risk profile and stakeholder expectations.

Progress indicators reported for 2024 include:

- **Scope 1 and 2**: 40.0% progress
- **Scope 3 Category 15**: 25.0% progress
- **Net zero (all scopes)**: 13.3% progress

These indicators are used to track progress across operational and financed emissions dimensions.

### Trade-offs and strategic choices

The bank’s climate strategy involves balancing risk reduction and opportunity capture across different parts of the portfolio:

- **Transition risk management vs. client franchise**: Stakeholder and NGO scrutiny of financed emissions and fossil financing could damage brand and trigger client attrition, reinforcing the strategic importance of financed-emissions management and portfolio steering.
- **Collateral value protection vs. borrower affordability**: Building energy performance regulation may require costly retrofits for low-EPC mortgage collateral, which can reduce collateral values and increase loan-to-value; this creates a strategic need to monitor EPC-related risk while maintaining prudent credit decisions.
- **Orderly vs. disorderly transition pathways**: Scenario analysis indicates that disorderly transition pathways can increase capital consumption from stranded asset impairments in the medium term, while orderly pathways are assessed as more manageable within existing buffers.

### Climate-related scenario analysis and resilience

The bank uses climate-related scenario analysis to assess strategic resilience across transition and physical risk pathways.

#### Scenario set and scope

- **Scope of analysis**: The analysis covers **all banking book exposures across DE, FR, IT, NL and ES**.
- **Orderly transition scenarios**: The bank applies **NGFS v4 Net Zero 2050** using a top-down sector-pathway approach. Carbon price assumptions are mapped from the **IEA NZE 2050** trajectory to counterparty NACE sectors, and stranded asset estimates are derived from sector-level fossil fuel capex exposure.
- **Orderly below-2°C scenario**: The bank applies **NGFS v4 Below 2°C (orderly)** aligned to a 1.8°C median temperature pathway, using a carbon price trajectory from the REMIND-MAgPIE model. Financial impact is assessed through loan-level probability of default sensitivity to carbon cost pass-through by sector carbon intensity tier.
- **Disorderly transition scenario**: The bank uses **NGFS v4 Divergent Net Zero** to capture technology pathway uncertainty, with carbon price assumptions varying by country based on national ETS and carbon tax trajectories. This scenario is used primarily to stress-test technology transition risk in the automotive and energy manufacturing lending sub-portfolio.
- **Delayed policy action scenario**: The bank uses **NGFS v4 NDC**, representing delayed but eventually strengthened policy action. Carbon pricing ramp-up is assumed from 2030 onward based on Paris Agreement NDC submission trajectories. Revenue-at-risk is computed from sector revenue sensitivity to carbon price at scenario-specified EUR/tCO₂e levels.
- **Hot house world (current policies) scenario**: The bank uses **NGFS v4 Current Policies** as an adverse baseline. Physical risk parameters are sourced from NGFS chronic and acute hazard layers and downscaled to counterparty postcode and collateral location, using RCP 8.5 physical risk projections for a 2050 horizon analysis.

#### Resilience assessment outcomes

- **Orderly transition resilience**: Under orderly transition scenarios, the portfolio demonstrates **adequate resilience**. Transition risk losses remain within **Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds**. Green loan growth and client engagement on transition plans are expected to progressively reduce high-carbon sector concentration over the medium term.
- **Disorderly transition resilience**: Under disorderly transition scenarios, near-term resilience is assessed as **acceptable**, but **medium-term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is material**. Sector exposure limits and active portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring are identified as the primary resilience mechanisms. Additional capital buffers may be required post-2030 under the most adverse disorderly assumptions.
- **Hot house world resilience**: Under hot house world scenarios, **physical risk losses** represent the dominant long-term resilience challenge. Collateral revaluation risk in flood-exposed mortgage books and agricultural borrower default risk from chronic heat stress are identified as primary transmission channels. The bank’s flood-zone overlay and EPC-based collateral monitoring are described as resilience mechanisms deployed for the 2050 horizon.

- **€9,214.0m** (medium-term horizon)

# 4. Risk Management

This section describes how climate-related risks are identified, assessed, prioritised and monitored, and how these processes are integrated into the organisation’s overall risk management.

### Risk identification and assessment

**Purpose.** The objective of our climate risk management process is to maintain a consistent, decision-useful view of climate-related risks across the organisation and value chain, supported by defined inputs, scenario references and a structured rating methodology.

#### Identify

Climate-related risks are captured and maintained in a **climate risk register**. The register records, for each risk, key attributes used in risk management, including a risk description (where applicable), a likelihood score, a risk rating, a monitoring cadence, and—where relevant—a reference to the scenario analysis used to inform the risk’s identification.

Inputs used to inform identification include:

- **External physical risk indicators** sourced from **ECB climate indicators** (used as a data source for physical risk exposure inputs).
- **Value chain mapping** that highlights where climate-related risks can arise across operations and financing activities. For example:
  - Operational sites and travel are identified as generating Scope 1–2 emissions and facing **physical disruption**.
  - Lending to **other mining and quarrying** is identified as carrying **elevated transition risk** from carbon pricing and demand shifts.

These inputs support a structured identification process that considers both operational exposure (e.g., physical disruption affecting sites and travel) and financed exposure (e.g., transition risk in carbon-intensive sectors).

#### Assess

**Purpose.** Assessment is designed to evaluate the nature of climate-related risks and to support consistent comparison and escalation through a common scoring approach.

Risks in the climate risk register are assessed using a **5×5 risk matrix** that combines likelihood and severity. The risk rating is derived by multiplying the **likelihood score** by a **severity score** to produce a composite score.

Risk categories are defined as follows:

- **Critical**: composite scores of **15–25**
- **High**: composite scores of **8–12**
- **Medium**: composite scores of **3–6**
- **Low**: composite scores of **1–2**

Likelihood is explicitly captured in the register (examples of likelihood scores recorded include **3**, **4** and **5**). Where a risk rating is recorded, the register includes the resulting category (for example, several risks are rated **medium**).

Scenario analysis is used to inform identification and assessment for selected risks. The climate risk register includes explicit links to scenario analysis references, including **SCN0005**, **SCN0007**, **SCN0009** and **SCN0011**. These references provide traceability between the risk register and the scenario analysis work used to inform the risk’s framing.

A representative example of a climate-related risk captured in the register is:

- **Chronic heat and shifting precipitation reduce yields for agricultural counterparties, weakening repayment capacity over the medium term.**

This example illustrates how physical climate drivers can translate into credit-related impacts through counterparties’ operating performance.

#### Prioritise

**Purpose.** Prioritisation supports consistent focus on the most significant climate-related risks and aligns climate risk management with enterprise-wide risk decision-making.

Climate-related risks are prioritised using the risk rating categories described above (low, medium, high and critical), derived from the 5×5 matrix. This enables risks to be compared on a consistent basis and supports escalation and management attention in line with the assessed rating.

The climate risk register also records whether individual risks have **changed since the prior reporting period**, supporting prioritisation and management focus on risks with evolving profiles.

### Monitoring

**Purpose.** Monitoring is designed to track changes in risk profile and support timely management response through defined review cadences.

Each risk in the climate risk register is assigned a **monitoring frequency**, reflecting the expected pace of change and the need for oversight. Monitoring cadences recorded in the register include:

- **Monthly** monitoring for selected risks
- **Quarterly** monitoring for selected risks
- **Semi-annual** monitoring for selected risks

This cadence-based approach supports ongoing review of risk drivers, including those informed by external indicators (such as ECB climate indicators for physical risk exposures) and scenario analysis references linked to specific risks.

### Integration into enterprise risk management

**Purpose.** Integration ensures climate-related risks are managed through the same overarching risk management architecture used for other principal risks, supporting consistent governance, escalation and reporting.

Climate-related risks are integrated into overall risk management through the climate risk register’s linkage to enterprise risk management. For many risks recorded in the register, climate risk management is explicitly marked as **integrated into enterprise risk management**.

The register-based approach supports integration in practice by:

- Applying a consistent **risk rating methodology** (likelihood and severity combined through the 5×5 matrix) to enable comparability and prioritisation.
- Maintaining traceability to **scenario analysis references** for risks where scenario analysis informs identification.
- Assigning defined **monitoring frequencies** to support routine oversight and escalation through established risk management rhythms.

While integration is applied broadly, the register also distinguishes risks that are not marked as integrated into enterprise risk management, supporting transparency in how climate-related risks are embedded across the risk management system.

### Value chain considerations in risk management

**Purpose.** Considering the value chain supports a more complete view of climate-related risks, including those arising from operations and from financing activities.

Climate-related risk identification and monitoring are informed by value chain mapping that highlights representative risk themes across different segments. Examples include:

- **Operations (sites and travel):** exposure to **physical disruption**, alongside emissions associated with Scope 1–2.
- **Financing activities (lending):** exposure to **transition risk** in sectors such as **other mining and quarrying**, driven by carbon pricing and demand shifts.

These examples illustrate how the risk management process considers climate-related risks across both operational activities and the broader value chain, and how such risks are captured in the climate risk register for assessment, prioritisation and monitoring.

### Target TGT001 — Scope 1 and Scope 2 absolute reduction

- **Target type:** Absolute reduction
- **Metric used to set and track the target:** tCO₂e (absolute)
