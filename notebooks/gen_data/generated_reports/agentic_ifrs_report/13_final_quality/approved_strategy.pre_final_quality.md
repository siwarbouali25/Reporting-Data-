## Strategy

This Strategy section explains how climate-related risks and opportunities affect the bank’s business model and value chain, and how the bank responds across the short term, medium term and long term.

### Climate-related risks and opportunities and their expected time horizons

The bank’s climate-related risk profile is driven primarily by exposures in the banking book, where climate factors can affect counterparty creditworthiness, collateral values and client behaviour. Key climate-related risks identified include:

- **Physical risk — mortgage book flood exposure**: Residential and commercial mortgage collateral located in flood-prone zones faces rising acute physical risk. Property damage and devaluation could impair collateral coverage.
- **Physical risk — wildfire exposure in southern portfolio (risk rating: medium)**.
- **Transition risk — building energy performance regulation (risk rating: medium)**: Minimum energy performance standards may require costly retrofits for low-energy-performance mortgage collateral, reducing values and increasing loan-to-value.
- **Transition risk — carbon pricing and EU ETS tightening**: Tightening EU ETS and national carbon pricing raise operating costs for carbon-intensive borrowers, increasing default probability in cement, steel and chemicals.
- **Stranded assets — fossil fuel sector exposure**: Demand shifts away from fossil fuels risk stranding reserves and assets held by oil, gas and coal counterparties, reducing their enterprise value.
- **Technology transition risk**: Rapid electrification could strand internal-combustion vehicle manufacturers and suppliers in the lending book.
- **Reputational and client franchise risk**: Stakeholder and NGO scrutiny of financed emissions and fossil financing could damage brand and trigger client attrition.
- **Physical risk affecting agricultural counterparties (medium term)**: Chronic heat and shifting precipitation reduce yields for agricultural counterparties, weakening repayment capacity over the medium term.

Climate-related opportunities identified include:

- **Sustainable bond underwriting (short term)**.
- **Green loan growth (medium term)**.
- **Transition advisory**: Fee income from climate risk advisory, transition planning support, and ESG due diligence services offered to large corporate clients.
- **Renewable energy project finance**: Project finance for solar, wind, and battery storage infrastructure aligned with REPowerEU and national renewable energy targets.

### Effects on the value chain and where climate risk concentrates

The bank’s climate-related impacts on enterprise value arise across its own operations and, more significantly, through financed activities.

- **Own operations**: Operational sites and travel generate Scope 1–2 emissions and face physical disruption. Corporate fleet and business travel are identified as part of the bank’s own activities with this risk profile.
- **Financing counterparties (banking book)**: Climate risk is concentrated in financed exposures where transition and physical drivers can transmit into credit risk and collateral risk. Examples of climate-risk concentration in the financing value chain include:
  - Lending to **manufacture of coke and refined petroleum**, which carries elevated transition risk from carbon pricing and demand shifts.
  - Exposure to **manufacture of motor vehicles**, which is subject to moderate physical and transition risk.

These value-chain characteristics align with the bank’s scenario analysis scope, which covers **all banking book exposures across DE, FR, IT, NL and ES**.

### Strategic response: how the bank positions the business model

The bank’s strategy addresses climate-related risks and opportunities through portfolio positioning, client engagement and product development, with a focus on the banking book.

**Portfolio and client strategy (banking book)**

- Under **orderly transition scenarios**, the bank expects **green loan growth** and **client engagement on transition plans** to progressively reduce high-carbon sector concentration over the medium term.
- Under **disorderly transition scenarios**, the bank identifies **sector exposure limits** and **active portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring** as primary resilience mechanisms.
- For **long-term physical risk**, the bank identifies **flood-zone overlay** and **energy performance certificate (EPC)-based collateral monitoring** as resilience mechanisms deployed for the 2050 horizon, reflecting the importance of collateral revaluation risk in flood-exposed mortgage books and default risk in agricultural borrowers under chronic heat stress.

**Opportunity-led growth**

The bank’s opportunity set is aligned to client demand for transition and climate solutions:

- In the **short term**, the bank identifies **sustainable bond underwriting** as an opportunity.
- In the **medium term**, the bank identifies **green loan growth** as an opportunity.
- The bank also identifies opportunities to generate fee income through **transition advisory** services (climate risk advisory, transition planning support and ESG due diligence) and to expand **renewable energy project finance** for solar, wind and battery storage infrastructure.

### Trade-offs and strategic choices

The bank’s climate strategy involves balancing risk reduction with client and product opportunities in sectors undergoing transition.

- **Transition risk management vs. client franchise**: Stakeholder and NGO scrutiny of financed emissions and fossil financing can create reputational risk and client attrition risk, while demand for transition finance and advisory services creates revenue opportunities. The bank’s strategic response emphasises client engagement on transition plans and growth in green lending and sustainable finance activities.
- **Collateral protection vs. affordability and retrofit burden**: Building energy performance regulation can require costly retrofits for low-energy-performance mortgage collateral, potentially affecting collateral values and loan-to-value. The bank’s use of EPC-based collateral monitoring is positioned as a resilience mechanism for this risk channel.
- **Sector concentration vs. transition pathway uncertainty**: Disorderly transition scenarios highlight the potential for material medium-term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments. Sector exposure limits and portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring are used to manage this trade-off.

### Scenario analysis and resilience of the strategy

The bank uses climate scenario analysis to assess resilience across transition and physical risk pathways.

**Scenario set and scope**

- **NGFS v4 Net Zero 2050**: Applied to the banking book using a top-down sector-pathway approach. Carbon price assumptions from the IEA Net Zero Emissions 2050 trajectory are mapped to counterparty NACE sectors. Stranded asset estimates are derived from sector-level fossil fuel capex exposure.
- **NGFS v4 Below 2°C (orderly)**: Applied using NGFS integrated assessment model outputs aligned to a 1.8°C median temperature pathway. Carbon price trajectory is sourced from the REMIND-MAgPIE model. Financial impact is assessed through loan-level probability of default sensitivity to carbon cost pass-through by sector carbon intensity tier.
- **NGFS v4 Divergent Net Zero (disorderly/technology uncertainty)**: Used to capture technology pathway uncertainty, with divergent sectoral transition speeds across jurisdictions and carbon price assumptions varying by country based on national ETS and carbon tax trajectories. Used primarily to stress-test technology transition risk in the automotive and energy manufacturing lending sub-portfolio.
- **NGFS v4 NDC (delayed policy action)**: Represents delayed but eventually strengthened policy action, with carbon pricing ramp-up assumed from 2030 onward based on Paris Agreement NDC submission trajectories. Revenue-at-risk is computed from sector revenue sensitivity to carbon price at scenario-specified EUR/tCO2e levels.
- **NGFS v4 Current Policies (hot house world)**: Used as the adverse baseline for physical risk. Physical risk parameters are sourced from NGFS chronic and acute hazard layers, downscaled to counterparty postcode and collateral location, with RCP 8.5 physical risk projections used for the 2050 horizon analysis.

**Resilience conclusions by scenario type**

- **Orderly transition scenarios (medium term)**: The portfolio demonstrates **adequate resilience**. Transition risk losses remain **within Pillar 2 capital buffer thresholds**. The bank expects green loan growth and client engagement on transition plans to progressively reduce high-carbon sector concentration over the medium term.
- **Disorderly transition scenarios**: Near-term resilience is **acceptable**, but **medium-term capital consumption from stranded asset impairments is material**. Sector exposure limits and active portfolio decarbonisation glide-path monitoring are identified as the primary resilience mechanisms. Under the most adverse disorderly assumptions, **additional capital buffers may be required post-2030**.
- **Hot house world scenarios (long term)**: Physical risk losses represent the **dominant long-term resilience challenge**. The primary transmission channels identified are **collateral revaluation risk in flood-exposed mortgage books** and **agricultural borrower default risk from chronic heat stress**. Flood-zone overlay and EPC-based collateral monitoring are identified as resilience mechanisms deployed for the 2050 horizon.
